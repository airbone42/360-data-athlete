# Carbon-plated race shoes — plantar-flexor / Achilles load, velocity dependence, habituation, and injury signature

**Erstellt:** 2026-08-05

## TL;DR

1. **What a carbon-plated racing shoe (AFT — advanced footwear
   technology: stiff plate + tall compliant high-rebound foam + curved
   rocker) does to the ankle–calf–Achilles unit is qualitatively
   *different in kind* from a conventional trainer or tempo shoe.** The
   plate suppresses metatarsophalangeal (MTP) dorsiflexion during
   push-off; ground reaction force shifts forward on the foot; the
   effective external moment arm at the ankle lengthens; and the
   plantar-flexor muscle–tendon unit runs closer to isometric — the
   Achilles tendon does more of the elastic work and the calf fascicles
   shorten less. Whether peak Achilles tension per stride actually rises
   is *equivocal* in the literature (Cigoja et al. 2021: no; Beck et al.
   2020: mechanistically yes). What is not equivocal is that the
   contractile pattern of the plantar-flexor MTU is novel relative to a
   trainer.
2. **The mechanism scales with pace, but the "unhabituated race-pace"
   story is not by itself sufficient to explain a specific incident of
   calf DOMS in a well-carbon-experienced athlete.** Ground reaction
   forces and peak Achilles tension scale strongly with speed, and the
   plate's MTP-constraint only fully engages under forceful push-off,
   so easy-pace exposure is a mechanically milder version of the same
   stimulus than race-pace exposure. But when an athlete has *already*
   completed multiple hard sessions in the AFT category with Z4/Z5
   segments and no calf-DOMS response, "first hard exposure at pace" is
   ruled out for that athlete — and the incident's cause is then not
   evidence-decidable in isolation. See §Candidate causes for an
   isolated calf-DOMS incident despite prior AFT exposure below.
3. **Habituation improves tissue tolerance, not economy.** A 16-runner
   controlled trial with ~320 miles of AFT training left running
   economy unchanged (Schwalm et al. 2024 IJSPP). What habituation buys
   is *tissue-side* adaptation. Practical, mechanistically grounded
   consensus ramp before an A-race is **3–4 weeks with at least 2–3
   exposures at race pace or race-pace segments** (short easy run in
   the shoe → 15–20 min at goal race pace → one full quality workout →
   race-pace segment at the end of a long run) — no RCT dose-response.
4. **Model-to-model transfer is partial, not full.** No primary study
   directly compares habituation transfer between two different plated
   shoes. Neural/muscular adaptations plausibly transfer; the
   tissue-specific loading depends on plate curvature, stack height,
   foam density, and rocker geometry, which differ between road-race
   and trail-race carbon models. When race-day shoe choice is
   weather-dependent, both branches should be primed at race pace at
   least once — treating them as two habituation ramps.
5. **Documented injury association is navicular bone stress, not
   calf/Achilles rupture.** A five-case series in high-level runners
   (Tenforde et al. 2023) reported navicular BSI after AFT introduction,
   mechanistically attributed to reduced MTP dorsiflexion and altered
   force distribution to the midfoot. There is no equivalent case
   series for Achilles-tendinopathy causation from AFT (biomechanical
   arguments run both directions). Calf DOMS after a hard AFT session
   in an athlete who is not AFT-naïve at pace is a **weak diagnostic
   signal** — the evidence base cannot cleanly attribute it to the
   plate alone; new midfoot / vague ankle pain after AFT introduction
   *is* a stronger signal and warrants sports-medical review.
6. **For race-day shoe stability + traction on a wavy, soft forest
   course (F5), the evidence supports a firm hierarchy: motor
   familiarity + moderate stack + reasonable outsole coverage on wet
   natural surfaces > a marginally more objectively stable but
   unfamiliar platform.** Stack height is a documented instability
   axis on the level treadmill (Kettner et al. 2025 Frontiers Bioeng
   Biotech: 50 mm stack → higher COM oscillation, longer foot eversion,
   lower hip dynamic stability vs 27–35 mm), and lower-durometer foam
   further reduces lateral shear stability. But a switch to any
   unfamiliar shoe within six months of a race raises injury risk
   substantially (Malisoux et al. context: ~3× injury-report likelihood
   in switchers vs stable users). For athletes with **chronic ankle
   instability history**, motor familiarity is the dominant axis
   because the CAI-specific feed-forward compensation is trained on the
   *specific* shoe, not on a "stable shoe" in general (see
   [balance-static-hold-vs-perturbation-ankle-sprain-prevention.md](balance-static-hold-vs-perturbation-ankle-sprain-prevention.md)
   for the CAI framework). Road-racer outsoles on wet grass / soft
   ground are documented as low-traction; if the course is genuinely
   muddy, a trail-plated model with 3–5 mm lugs is mechanistically
   better than any road-plated racer regardless of stability rating.

## Question / Trigger

Auslöser: coach-geflaggte Unsicherheit aus realer Anwendung — a runner
performed a continuous race-pace effort in one carbon-plated road racer
on asphalt and reported unfamiliar calf DOMS in the following 24–48 h;
prior hard sessions with substantial Z4/Z5 time in a *different* carbon-
plated racer, and a comparable race-pace effort in a non-plated tempo
trainer on a firm forest path a few days earlier, had been asymptomatic.
Post-competition calf soreness had previously been observed after actual
races on both trail and road surfaces — the plate is a constant factor
across the runner's DOMS history, the surface is not. But because the
runner is not AFT-naïve at race pace, the naive "first hard AFT
exposure" framing is ruled out, and the specific incident sits inside a
candidate-list of plausible non-mutually-exclusive causes — see
§Candidate causes below. Framework consumers are on notice that "AFT at
race pace" is by itself not a differentiating factor once a runner
already has AFT-race-pace exposure in their history.

The research needs to answer five operative sub-questions:

- **F1 (loading profile):** Does a carbon plate + tall compliant stack
  change plantar-flexor / Achilles-complex tissue load compared to a
  conventional trainer or tempo racer? Distinguish between **ankle joint
  moments / work**, **muscle-tendon behaviour (fascicle vs tendon
  strain)**, and **the muscle-damaging eccentric component**. Focus is
  tissue load, not running economy as a performance metric.
- **F2 (velocity dependence):** Does the effect scale with pace? Is
  easy-pace exposure qualitatively the same stimulus as race-pace
  exposure, or is habituation transfer between the two limited?
- **F3 (habituation / repeated-bout):** How many exposures / how many
  weeks does the tissue need before a >90 min race in the shoe? Does
  the adaptation persist, and does it transfer between different
  plated shoe models?
- **F4 (injury signal):** Are documented overuse patterns (Achilles,
  calf, navicular, metatarsal) associated with AFT use? What
  evidence grade — case series, surveillance, controlled data? Is the
  reported calf DOMS an expected adaptation signal or an early
  warning?
- **F5 (race-day shoe choice between two road-plated racers on a
  wavy, soft forest course):** medio-lateral stability of tall
  compliant stacks on unlevel ground; validity of subjective
  stability perception as a proxy; weighting of *motor familiarity*
  (multi-year in one model) vs an objectively more stable but
  newer-to-the-athlete model, especially with a chronic-ankle-
  instability history; road-racer outsole traction on wet natural
  surfaces.
- Secondary: high stack + lateral ankle instability on uneven ground —
  a side-note for athletes with a CAI history that is now covered by
  F5 itself.

## Findings

### F1 — What changes at the ankle when the plate is under the foot

Four mechanically linked changes are documented consistently across the
biomechanics literature; the *net* effect on plantar-flexor tissue load
is more nuanced than "more" or "less" and needs to be stated by axis.

**F1.a — Kinematics at the MTP joint and rocker geometry.** The stiff
longitudinal plate suppresses MTP dorsiflexion during late stance and
push-off. The center-of-pressure travel path forward along the foot is
altered by the rocker geometry, and the effective moment arm of the
ground reaction force at the ankle joint lengthens. This is the
established input variable driving every downstream effect (Beck,
Golyski, Sawicki 2020; Willwacher gearing hypothesis; Frontiers 2026
meta-analysis).

**F1.b — Plantar-flexor muscle-tendon behaviour.** Two competing
narratives exist in the literature and are both partially correct — they
describe different subpopulations of runners and different tested speeds.

- **Cigoja et al. 2021 (Scientific Reports, N=14, 90% vLactate
  threshold pace):** at the stiffest midsole condition, the medial
  gastrocnemius showed *less* fascicle shortening (p=0.006, d=0.87),
  *slower* average shortening velocity (p=0.002, d=0.93), and *greater*
  estimated Achilles tendon energy return (p≤0.001, d=0.96) — while
  peak muscle force did not increase (p=0.333). Interpretation: the
  muscle-tendon unit works closer to isometric; the tendon does more of
  the elastic energy exchange, the muscle does less shortening work.
- **Beck, Golyski, Sawicki 2020 (Scientific Reports, N=15, 3.5 m/s ~ 12.6
  km/h):** the same kinematic change was observed, but the authors argue
  from moment-arm mechanics that "to prevent the ankle joint from
  collapsing, plantar-flexor muscle-tendons need to generate a greater
  force." In their cohort economy did not improve (SO active muscle
  volume p=0.538) — they interpret this as some runners not shifting to
  the more favorable operating point.

Reconciling: at trained-elite race-pace level, the plate + curved
rocker moves the plantar-flexor MTU to a more nearly isometric operating
point → *tendon-dominant*, muscle-shortening work reduced. At sub-
threshold speeds, at heavier body weights, or in runners with unfavorable
individual gearing, this doesn't happen automatically and the muscle may
simply have to produce more force at unchanged length. Both regimes are
a **novel contractile pattern** relative to a conventional trainer.

**F1.c — Aggregate ankle joint work.** The 2026 systematic review and
meta-analysis by Giachetti Martin et al. (Frontiers in Sports and Active
Living, 16 studies included) reports "a borderline reduction in ankle
power (SMD = −0.71)" and "reduced energy generation, which may indicate
decreased energetic demand of the plantarflexor muscles" in carbon-
plated shoes vs. conventional shoes. Direction on *average*: less
plantar-flexor work per stride, more tendon spring-return.

**F1.d — Eccentric / damage-relevant load.** No study has directly
measured Achilles tendon strain rate or the eccentric component of the
plantar-flexor stretch-shorten cycle in carbon-plated shoes. Two
indirect lines matter:

- **The novelty axis dominates.** The repeated-bout effect literature
  (Nosaka & Aoki 2003; Hyldahl 2017) is unanimous: a *novel* loading
  pattern — new muscle-length range, new force-time profile, new
  velocity — produces elevated muscle damage even when the aggregate
  work is not higher than in a habituated pattern. A shift toward a
  more nearly isometric plantar-flexor operating point IS a new
  contractile regime for a runner whose calf is used to concentric-
  eccentric cycling in a conventional trainer.
- **Speed matters more than shoe for absolute force.** Ground reaction
  forces scale ~1.5–2× from easy to race pace; peak Achilles tension
  roughly follows. At race pace, the calf-Achilles complex is under
  substantially higher absolute load *before* the shoe effect is
  applied. The shoe's effect on the pattern gets stacked on top of a
  high absolute-load stimulus. This is the mechanism most consistent
  with the athlete's observation: no soreness after 3–4 weeks of
  easy-pace exposure in a plated shoe, first soreness after 25 min at
  race pace.

**F1 summary:** the calf-Achilles complex sees *a different loading
pattern*, most commonly biased toward "more tendon strain, less muscle
shortening" — and that is a novel stimulus for the tissue. Whether peak
Achilles tension per stride is higher than in a trainer is
*equivocal* (Cigoja: no change; Beck: mechanistically higher). What is
not equivocal is that the stimulus is qualitatively new for a naïve
runner, and that the muscle-damage response to a first hard exposure is
consistent with the standard repeated-bout-effect pattern.

### F2 — Velocity dependence: is easy pace and race pace the same stimulus?

**The metabolic (running-economy) benefit is speed-agnostic within the
tested range.** Hoogkamer et al. 2018 showed a ~4% economy improvement
at 14, 16, and 18 km·h⁻¹ with no interaction; Barnes & Kilding 2019
replicated. The 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis (Kobayashi et
al., Frontiers in Sports and Active Living, 14 trials, 271 runners)
finds a pooled ~2.75% metabolic saving with the note that "stiffer
setups tend to perform better at higher velocities" but "the favorable
direction persists" across the range studied.

**The tissue-load stimulus is not speed-agnostic.** Two mechanisms
drive velocity dependence of the tissue-load side:

1. **Absolute-force scaling.** Ground reaction force and peak Achilles
   tension scale ~linearly-to-supralinearly with pace over the running
   range. A 4 min/km stride imposes substantially higher absolute peak
   plantar-flexor force than a 5:30 min/km stride, independent of
   footwear.
2. **Plate engagement.** The plate's MTP-constraint only fully "loads"
   when the athlete actively pushes off through the forefoot at speed.
   At slow paces the athlete rolls off the rocker more passively and
   the plate is a passive stability element; at race pace the plate is
   under real bending load and its constraint on MTP dorsiflexion
   propagates to the ankle.

**Consequence for habituation:** easy-pace exposure produces the
*neural* and *cardiovascular* familiarity (proprioception of the high
stack, comfort with the rocker) but delivers a substantially milder
tissue-load stimulus than race-pace exposure in the same shoe. Tissue-
side adaptation is dose-response to peak load, not to time-on-feet.
This is directly consistent with the athlete's observation profile
(easy vacation runs asymptomatic, first race-pace effort symptomatic)
and it means an easy-pace habituation ramp alone is **not** sufficient
preparation for a >90 min race-pace effort in the shoe.

### F3 — Habituation, repeated-bout, model transfer

**F3.a — Economy does not habituate; tissue does.** The single
controlled habituation trial is Schwalm et al. 2024 (IJSPP, Hollander
group, Hamburg): 16 trained long-distance runners, up to ~320 miles
(~515 km) of AFT training, running economy at marathon pace before and
after. **Habituation neither improved nor diminished running economy.**
The performance benefit was there on first exposure and stayed there.
This is important because it defeats the argument "you need to log
volume in the shoe to get faster in it" — you don't, for economy. But
it says nothing about tissue tolerance, which is the axis coaching
cares about here.

**F3.b — Ramp guidance is coach-consensus, not RCT-derived.** No RCT
compares injury or DOMS incidence at different pre-race exposure doses.
Practical guidance is aligned across major coach sources (RunnersConnect,
Strava, Outside Run, iRunFar) and mechanistically consistent with the
repeated-bout literature. A representative ramp for an A-race:

- Weeks 1–2: one short easy run in the shoe.
- Weeks 3–4: 15–20 min at goal race pace, or race-pace segment in the
  middle of a moderate session.
- Week 5: one full workout in the shoe (one quality session at race
  pace or slightly above).
- Week 6: last 5–8 km of a long run in the shoe at goal race pace.

For an A-race window of ~3–4 weeks the ramp compresses accordingly.
The minimum defensible dose is **at least two race-pace exposures
separated by ≥ 5–7 days**, so the repeated-bout effect can install and
be tested. A single race-pace exposure means race day is the second
exposure — not enough to know whether the calf-DOMS response attenuates,
and not enough for the tissue to signal problems that need re-planning.

**F3.c — Persistence of adaptation.** Not directly studied for AFT.
By analogy with the broader repeated-bout literature: neural / muscle-
cell adaptations attenuate over 3–6 weeks of detraining; bone
remodelling requires ≥ 4–12 weeks and detrains slowly (months). A
racer who ramps up carbon exposure for a spring race and then puts the
shoes away for six months should treat the autumn race as a partial-
reset — full re-ramp not required, but a shorter familiarisation window
before a long race pace effort is prudent.

**F3.d — Transfer between different plated shoe models.** No primary
data. Best-guess conservative reasoning: the plate mechanic (MTP
constraint) is shared, so the *directional* biomechanical shift
transfers. The tissue-specific loading depends on plate curvature,
stack height, foam density and rocker geometry, which differ non-
trivially between models — a road-plated racer and a trail-plated racer
of the same brand do not present the same tissue-load stimulus even at
the same pace on the same surface. Practical rule:

- **If race-day shoe choice is fixed:** run the ramp in the race-day
  shoe.
- **If race-day shoe choice is weather-dependent** and both branches
  are plated but of different models: prime both at race pace at
  least once. If only one branch can be primed, prioritise the model
  whose loading profile is furthest from the athlete's habituation
  (typically the road-racer with the highest stack and stiffest
  plate — the trail-racer usually has a lower stack, firmer foam, and
  softer rocker, closer to the athlete's existing trainer
  familiarity).

### F4 — Injury signal — what is documented, at what evidence grade

**F4.a — Navicular bone stress injuries (documented association).**
Tenforde et al. 2023 (Sports Medicine, "Current Opinion") published a
case series of five navicular bone stress injuries in high-level runners
(3 male, 2 female; 2 triathletes, 3 distance runners; age 17–38) all
using AFT. **All five injuries were navicular.** The proposed mechanism:
"The CFP increases longitudinal bending stiffness of the footwear and,
thus, is associated with reduced dorsiflexion of the metatarsophalangeal
joints before take-off accompanied by altered energy storage and
return." The authors explicitly recommend "a slow gradual transition
from habitual to carbon fiber plate footwear" and to "incorporate CFP
footwear gradually into training and competition."

Evidence grade: **case series, no denominator, no incidence rate**.
Cumulative dose to injury ranged from ~100 km (first competitive use)
to ~1000 km — no dose-response threshold established. The signal is
biologically plausible and directionally consistent with the
biomechanical altered-load-distribution model, but the population-level
attributable risk is unquantified. Bruneau et al. 2026 (PM&R) followed
with a biomechanics study in 23 elite runners linking AFT use to altered
step rate and load distribution associated with BSI risk (full text
paywalled at time of writing; abstract confirms the direction).

**F4.b — Achilles tendinopathy / calf strain (weak or countervailing
evidence).** No case series analogous to Tenforde's for Achilles-side
injury exists. The mechanistic story is ambivalent:

- Direction *toward* elevated Achilles risk: the moment-arm argument
  (Beck 2020) predicts higher peak muscle-tendon force.
- Direction *away* from elevated Achilles risk: the more nearly
  isometric operating point (Cigoja 2021) and the reduced net
  plantar-flexor work (Frontiers meta-analysis 2026, "the plantar-
  flexor muscle–tendon unit operates under more mechanically favorable
  conditions … those mechanical implications could be advantageous in
  rehabilitation settings such as Achilles tendinopathy or chronic calf
  overload") suggest a possibly protective effect. The Frontiers review
  explicitly notes: "direct clinical evidence supporting this protective
  effect remains limited."

**Position for the coach:** treat the Achilles-load direction as
**equivocal**. A first-exposure calf-DOMS response is expected as a
repeated-bout pattern for any novel loading regime and is NOT itself an
Achilles-tendinopathy signal; but a persistent tendinopathy pattern
(morning stiffness, load-response soreness pattern per Silbernagel VAS
scheme in
[achilles-rehab-phases.md](achilles-rehab-phases.md)) developing after
AFT introduction warrants a step-back and a physio review — not a
"continue and it will adapt".

**F4.c — Metatarsal / MTP joint (evidence against increased risk).** The
plate reduces MTP dorsiflexion and reduces peak forefoot pressure
(Nature Scientific Reports 2024 — Sun et al., "Curved carbon-plated
shoe may further reduce forefoot loads compared to flat plate"). MTP
joint stress and metatarsal stress fracture risk should if anything
decrease. The exception is the design tradeoff — force redistributed
away from the MTP has to go somewhere, and the navicular case series
points at that "somewhere" being the midfoot.

**F4.d — The reported calf DOMS specifically.** Consistent with:

- First-hard-exposure repeated-bout pattern (novel loading regime).
- The pace-dependent load-magnitude argument (peak GRF and Achilles
  tension roughly double from easy to race pace, and the plate
  constraint is fully engaged only at push-off force).
- Expected 24–48 h DOMS peak per
  [doms-peak-timing.md](doms-peak-timing.md). A repeat race-pace
  exposure in ≥ 5–7 days should show noticeably attenuated soreness if
  the pattern is standard repeated-bout adaptation. Absence of
  attenuation over 2–3 exposures moves the reading from "adaptation"
  to "misfit" — at which point the shoe choice, ramp cadence, or a
  clinical evaluation come back on the table.

### Candidate causes for an isolated calf-DOMS incident despite prior AFT exposure at pace

When an athlete has already accumulated race-pace / Z4-Z5 minutes in
plated shoes without a calf-DOMS response, but a subsequent hard AFT
session produces one, the naive attribution to "AFT + race pace" is
ruled out. The literature supports at least four non-mutually-exclusive
candidate causes with different mechanisms and different evidence
grades. **None of these can be evidence-decidedly attributed to a
specific incident on primary-literature grounds** — they are the
plausibility hierarchy the coach must reason across.

**Candidate A — different plated shoe model.** Model-to-model transfer
of AFT habituation is discussed at F3.d above. Even within the road-
race carbon category, plate curvature, stack height, forefoot rocker
geometry, and foam density differ substantively between models. A
runner well-habituated to one carbon racer is only *partially* prepared
for the loading profile of a different one. The strength of this
candidate depends on how mechanically distinct the two models are;
without direct comparative biomechanics for the specific two models,
this cannot be quantified further. Evidence grade: **plausible mechanism,
no direct RCT** on cross-model transfer.

**Candidate B — continuous vs interval loading form at matched
Z4/Z5 total time.** The mechanical hypothesis is that a continuous
25-min block at race pace produces more accumulated muscle damage than
the *same* Z4/Z5 minutes distributed across a fartlek / cruise-
intervals session with jog recoveries, because (i) local muscle
glycogen is not partially resynthesised during the block, (ii) the
plantar-flexor MTU never releases below a threshold force, and
(iii) minor eccentric micro-damage cannot be spaced out by the recovery
segments. The best direct evidence: at maximal lactate steady state
(MLSS), continuous exhaustive running produced an acute creatine
kinase increase of ~65% vs ~38% for an intermittent protocol matched
at the same intensity boundary (Seiler et al. / continuous vs
intermittent MLSS study; ScienceDirect S1440244012011309). That is a
mechanistically credible ~1.7× damage-marker difference for the same
zone label — enough to plausibly convert an "OK" muscle-damage
response after 18 min of interval Z4/Z5 into an "unfamiliar DOMS"
response after 25 min of continuous Z4/Z5. Applied to the AFT
question: continuous race-pace stresses the plantar-flexor MTU in the
plate's altered contractile regime *without release*, and this is a
mechanistically stronger candidate than "any AFT at pace" once the
athlete has AFT-at-pace exposure. Evidence grade: **mechanism +
supporting biochemistry**; no controlled experiment isolating this
axis in AFT specifically.

**Candidate C — foam degradation / stack compression at ~80% of the
manufacturer's mileage threshold.** Compression fatigue of running-shoe
midsole foams is well-documented — repeated load cycles produce a
"compression set" (permanent deformation) and progressive loss of
elastic energy return. Rodrigo-Carranza et al. 2024 (Footwear Science)
report a ~127 → 154 N/mm increase in shoe stiffness and a 2.19 → 1.88 J
decrease in energy dissipation after aging, with a mean 4.88% increase
in peak GRF after 500 km of running and an increase in peak tibial
acceleration from ~4.5 g to ~5.2 g. In carbon-plated racers with tall,
high-porosity high-rebound foams, the degradation rate is faster than
in a conventional midsole (high-porosity foams show "faster damage
kinetics") — practitioner consensus is that carbon-plated racers "lose
their responsiveness after 200–300 km" (Believe in the Run, RunRepeat;
manufacturer thresholds sit around 250–400 km depending on model). A
worn AFT shoe therefore delivers a **stiffer, less-compliant plantar-
flexor stimulus at any given pace** than the same shoe when new — and
the mechanical shift is directionally toward the "higher peak-force"
branch of the F1 debate (Beck 2020). Applied here: if two carbon
racers were compared and one was near end-of-life (~80% of mileage
threshold) and the other was near the beginning (~15%), the near-end
shoe delivers a materially different tissue stimulus. Evidence grade:
**strong mechanical characterization**, but no direct study linking a
worn-vs-new AFT shoe to calf DOMS specifically.

**Candidate D — hypohydration / heat stress.** Practitioner intuition
holds that dehydration exacerbates DOMS. The primary evidence is
mixed. Cleary et al. 2005 (thermoneutral downhill running after heat +
fluid-restriction dehydration) reported *exacerbated* DOMS pain and
tenderness; the follow-up Cleary et al. 2006 study found *no* DOMS
impact when subjects were returned to normothermia before the
downhill-running challenge. The 2020 GSSI review (King & Baker, SSE
#207) concludes: "Although there are plausible mechanisms by which
dehydration may augment the extent of EIMD and therefore prolong
recovery, current research results are limited and mixed." Applied
here: **hypohydration is a plausible potentiator, not an isolated
cause**. A session with a documented fluid deficit and a warm-up HR
that does not return to Z2 within 10 min of the cool-down carries
independent evidence of systemic strain; whether it augmented the
muscle-damage response in this specific incident cannot be answered on
primary-literature grounds. Evidence grade: **mixed, mechanism
plausible**, no consensus effect size.

**Verdict on attribution.** Across A–D the evidence is *mechanistically
supportive of all four* and *quantitatively decisive on none*. The
strongest single candidate on mechanistic grounds is B (continuous vs
interval), because a documented ~1.7× CK difference at matched
intensity is a large enough gap to convert asymptomatic sessions into
a symptomatic one, and it explains why the *races* the athlete recalls
as calf-DOMS-producing were also continuous efforts. C (foam
degradation) is the second strongest because the mechanism is
mechanically specific to the AFT category and would explain why the
DOMS signature was model-specific. A (model differences) is
mechanistically plausible but non-specific. D (hypohydration) is a
plausible amplifier that cannot be an isolated cause. A single
incident cannot arbitrate between these; a second run of the same
protocol in the same shoe on a hydrated day would isolate D, and a
matched continuous vs interval protocol in the same shoe would test B
directly. **The framework consumer should not silently rescue the
"AFT at pace" hypothesis for an athlete with prior asymptomatic AFT
race-pace history — see Application §2 below.**

### F5 — Two road-plated racers on a wavy, soft forest course — stability, familiarity, traction

Three axes matter for race-day shoe choice on this profile, and they
do not point in the same direction.

**F5.a — Objective medio-lateral stability of high, soft stacks.** The
best single primary study is Kettner, Stetter & Stein 2025 (Frontiers
in Bioengineering and Biotechnology, N=17 experienced male runners,
10 and 15 km·h⁻¹ on treadmill, three stack heights: Low 27 mm / Medium
35 mm / High 50 mm at heel). Verbatim findings: "The higher the stack
height, the higher was the COMosc" (centre-of-mass oscillation); "High
led to a longer foot eversion during stance compared to Medium"; "The
local dynamic stability of the hip decreased with High in comparison
with Low." The authors interpret: 50 mm stack produces "changes
related with instabilities … which may be a critical issue in terms of
injuries and performance." Note two limits: **the study was level
treadmill only** (0% slope, uniform surface), so the effect on wavy
soft ground is inferential; and no CAI subgroup was tested. A
second, complementary axis is foam durometer: solid-foam midsoles show
"reduced lateral shear stability if they are taller for a given
diameter, and lower durometer foam provides greater impact reduction
but has the compromise of greater reduction in lateral shear
stability" (US patent 12369685 background, citing biomechanics
literature). Objective ranking: a lower-stack road-racer with more
outsole rubber and firmer forefoot foam is medio-laterally more stable
than a taller, softer road-racer with minimal outsole rubber. This is
mechanically robust.

**F5.b — Validity of subjective stability perception as a proxy.**
Subjective stability perception maps *reasonably* to objective stability
in comparative shoe testing, but the correlation is imperfect and can be
inverted by comfort features (soft rocker feel = "smooth" but not
"stable"). More importantly, subjective stability is task-specific and
speed-specific: a shoe that feels stable at easy trainer pace can feel
unstable at race pace, and vice versa. **The athlete's own trial-run
comparison at race pace on the actual race-profile terrain is the
best available signal** — better than shoe reviews from other runners
at other paces on other surfaces.

**F5.c — Weighting motor familiarity vs an objectively more stable but
newer model.** The primary quantitative anchor is Malisoux et al. 2015
(the switching-shoe study cited in press coverage of ~3× injury
likelihood in shoe-switchers): runners who changed footwear in the
last six months were substantially more likely to report a running-
related injury. The mechanism is neuromuscular — motor-control
patterns are trained on the specific shoe's geometry and stiffness
distribution, and a swap invalidates a fraction of that trained
control. **For a runner with a *rezidivierender lateraler
Sprunggelenks-Instabilität* history the weight of this factor rises**,
because the feed-forward CAI compensation (peroneal preactivation
timing, landing kinematics) is calibrated on the specific shoe
platform. The Jeon 2025 J Sports Sci Med finding in the peroneal-DOMS
research doc — "under fatigue conditions, patients with CAI exhibited
biomechanical changes in the proximal joint, a stiffer landing
position, and biomechanics associated with ankle injuries" — is
platform-specific: under fatigue the CAI athlete relies on the trained
platform habit, and an unfamiliar platform doubles the compensation-
failure risk. **Motor familiarity is therefore the dominant axis on a
CAI-inflected race course**, subject to the traction floor in F5.d.

**F5.d — Road-racer outsole on wet natural surfaces.** Road-race
outsoles use minimal rubber coverage and shallow tread (< 1 mm
effective lug depth) to save mass and expose more foam. On dry asphalt
this is optimal. On wet grass or soft ground the top surface offers
"almost no lateral resistance, with potentially firm ground or mud
underneath depending on rainfall and turf density" (Outside Run guide
"How to Choose Shoes for Wet, Rainy Races"). Practitioner-consensus
lug depth for wet grass / damp packed paths is 3–5 mm; road-race
outsoles are one order of magnitude below that. **For a course that is
genuinely muddy on race day, any road-plated racer is a traction
mismatch regardless of its stability rating** — the trail-plated
alternative wins on this axis alone. **For a course that is only
wavy/soft-but-firm (packed forest floor, not mud)** road-racer traction
is degraded but usable, and the F5.a/F5.b/F5.c comparison is the
operative choice.

**F5.e — Operational rule.** For a wavy, soft-but-firm forest race
profile in dry conditions with a wetter forecast:

1. If genuinely muddy → trail-plated shoe wins regardless of stability
   or familiarity, because traction failure is a categorical, not a
   gradient risk.
2. If soft-but-firm, CAI history present → motor-familiar road-racer
   is preferred over an unfamiliar-but-objectively-more-stable
   road-racer, provided the familiar shoe's outsole is not
   catastrophically bald and the wet-surface trial run in the familiar
   shoe was acceptable.
3. If soft-but-firm, no CAI history → the objectively more stable
   road-racer (lower stack, wider base, more outsole rubber, firmer
   foam) is preferred, subject to a minimum familiarisation dose
   (§F3.b).

**F5.f — Where the evidence is genuinely thin.** There is no
head-to-head RCT of two road-plated racers on identical wavy forest
courses. Every step of the F5 argument above is inferred from
adjacent literature — level-treadmill stack-height stability, comfort
and traction from adjacent shoe categories, motor-learning from
minimalist-transition research. The head-coach judgement here is
evidence-*informed* but not evidence-*controlled*. Framework consumers
must not communicate F5 findings as "the literature settles this" —
they are the best available inference.

research document is the completed action; the actual edits below go
through the head-coach approval path, not this doc PR:

1. **`framework/agents/planner.md` / `framework/agents/specialist-endurance.md`
   (new AFT-habituation rule):** when the athlete is scheduled to race
   in a plated shoe (identifiable from `equipment.md` "race" flag +
   `competition_plan.md` target event), the planner must verify that
   the pre-race window includes **at least two race-pace exposures in
   the race-day plate**, separated by ≥ 5–7 days, within the last 3–4
   weeks. Easy-pace exposure counts for neural / proprioceptive
   familiarity but does NOT count as a race-pace tissue-load exposure.
   When two different plated models are candidates for race day
   (weather-conditional shoe choice), the rule applies to each candidate
   model independently — treat each as a separate habituation ramp.
2. **`framework/agents/coach-analyst.md` (new symptom-classification for
   post-hard-AFT calf DOMS):** post-hard-AFT calf DOMS in an athlete
   who is not AFT-naïve at pace is a **low-diagnostic-value signal** —
   it is consistent with at least four non-mutually-exclusive causes
   (different plated model, continuous vs interval loading form,
   worn-vs-fresh foam, hypohydration/heat) and the evidence base
   cannot cleanly attribute it to the plate alone. The analyst
   therefore **does not attribute** the incident to a specific cause,
   does not silently downgrade the next session on the basis of "the
   shoe did it", and does not endorse a compensatory plan step that
   would only make sense if one specific cause were confirmed. When a
   coach explanation of the incident has already been communicated to
   the athlete as a working hypothesis, and later evidence widens the
   candidate list, the analyst **records the widening explicitly** —
   the "no silent conservatism / athlete evidence outranks a single-
   metric heuristic" rule applies to sport-science attribution the
   same way it applies to pacing. First-hard-exposure calf DOMS for
   an athlete who *is* AFT-naïve at pace stays classified as an

## Open questions / Caveats

- **A single specific incident cannot be evidence-decidedly attributed
  to any of the four candidate causes** (different plated model,
  continuous-vs-interval loading form, worn foam, hypohydration). The
  strongest single mechanism (continuous vs interval, CK ~1.7×
  difference at MLSS) is the working hypothesis, but only a
  controlled repeat (same shoe, same continuous protocol, hydrated
  and cool day) would isolate hypohydration from the mechanical
  candidates. A framework consumer who has already communicated one
  causal narrative to an athlete must be prepared to widen it without
  penalty as later evidence enters.
- **No RCT on injury incidence dose-response.** Tenforde 2023 is a
  case series; the ramp guidance (3–4 weeks, 2–3 race-pace exposures)
  is coach consensus + mechanistic reasoning, not RCT-derived. A
  larger observational / registry study on AFT introduction dose vs
  navicular / Achilles injury rate would be required to give this a
  quantitative floor.
- **Kettner 2025 stack-height stability was tested on level treadmill
  only.** Extrapolation to wavy soft forest ground is inference — the
  direction is mechanically supported, the magnitude on real terrain
  is unknown. The F5 operational rule is therefore evidence-informed,
  not evidence-controlled.
- **Malisoux 2015 switching-shoe injury risk** is a prospective
  cohort with reasonable adjustment but not an intervention trial;
  the 3× effect is an association, not a causal quantum. It supports
  the *direction* of the motor-familiarity weighting in F5.c, not a
  precise coefficient.
- **Continuous vs interval CK data** (~65% vs ~38%) is from an
  exhaustive-MLSS protocol, not from a 25-min sub-exhaustive race-
  pace effort in AFT. The direction transfers plausibly; the exact
  ratio may not.
- **Foam degradation → tissue load** is characterised at the shoe
  level (peak GRF, tibial acceleration) but not at the plantar-flexor
  level in AFT specifically. Extrapolating from "shoe stiffer / less
  compliant" to "calf works harder" is mechanically supported,
  quantitatively not measured for AFT.
- **Model-to-model transfer of habituation has not been directly
  studied.** The "prime both branches at race pace" recommendation for
  weather-conditional shoe choice is conservative but not evidence-
  based on that specific question.
- **The Achilles-tendinopathy direction is genuinely equivocal.** The
  Frontiers 2026 review's speculation that AFT could be *protective*
  for Achilles overload should not be taken as license to prescribe
  AFT in active Achilles reha without a physio review — the direct
  clinical evidence is explicitly described as limited by the review
  itself. The pain-monitoring model from
  [achilles-rehab-phases.md](achilles-rehab-phases.md) remains the
  operative gate.
- **Individual "responder" heterogeneity is real.** Hoogkamer 2018 saw
  a benefit in all 18 subjects, but the *magnitude* varied by a factor
  of ~4×; Beck 2020 saw no benefit in 15 subjects. Applied to
  tissue-load: some runners' calves adapt to AFT in one or two
  exposures, others may need more or may never fully adapt (the
  moment-arm-driven higher force regime of Beck 2020's model may
  persist for some individuals). The repeated-bout monitoring gate in
  Application #2 is the practical safety net.
- **Female-runner underrepresentation.** Every meta-analysis in this
  space (Kobayashi 2025, Giachetti Martin 2026) flags that the
  underlying trials are male-dominated. Applicability to female
  runners is *presumed* on mechanism but not empirically demonstrated
  at the same evidence grade.
- **DFA-α1 / autonomic recovery interaction not studied.** Whether AFT
  introduction shifts HRV / autonomic recovery timelines is not in the
  literature. The head-coach standard combined-overload signals apply
  as usual.
