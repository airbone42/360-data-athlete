# Downhill pace deviation and eccentric braking load — is there an operationalisable pace threshold?

**Erstellt:** 2026-08-29

## TL;DR

**No. There is no evidence-based pace threshold for downhill running, and the
premise behind one is contradicted by the only two datasets that crossed
running speed with downhill gradient.** Per-contact eccentric work rises
**sub**-proportionally with speed at a fixed decline and *plateaus* on steeper
grades (Khassetarash 2020: +67 % speed → +30 % absorbed energy at −10°, knee
absorption flat at 42.8 → 41.7 J; Robinson 2025 at −7.5°: stance knee eccentric
work 0.622 / 0.606 / 0.629 J·kg⁻¹ across three speeds). Pace is also a
*confounded* proxy: at identical downhill pace, a low-cadence / long-step
runner and a high-cadence / short-step runner differ by ~17 % in step length
and 11–15 % in vertical impulse (Vincent 2019). The load-bearing variables are
**gradient** (dose) and **step length / foot-landing distance ahead of the hip**
(per-contact lever) — the latter being the *stronger direct predictor of
braking impulse* than stride frequency itself (Lieberman 2015, β = 0.89 vs
−0.59). Both are readable during a run: gradient from the watch, **stride
length** as a live Garmin running-dynamics data field. The operative rule is
therefore step-length-anchored, not pace-anchored — which matters because the
runner's *default* way of getting faster downhill is precisely to lengthen the
step (Robinson 2025, Sundström 2021), i.e. the one lever that loads the knee
most (Baggaley 2020).

## Question / Trigger

Auslöser: coach-geflaggte Unsicherheit aus realer Anwendung. Für Athletinnen
und Athleten mit einem Limiter, der auf **exzentrische Bremslast** reagiert
(Patellofemoralschmerz, Quadrizeps-DOMS, Achillessehnen-Reha, lumbosakrale
Reizung), wird auf welligen Kursen eine Gefälle-Deckelung ausgesprochen. Die
qualitative Fassung ("kurzer Schritt, Kadenz oben, Fuß landet unter dem
Körper") ist unstrittig. Offen war, ob es dafür eine **operationalisierbare,
auf der Uhr ablesbare Größe** gibt — konkret, ob eine Pace-basierte Schwelle
("maximal X s/km unter Abschnitts-Pace") literaturgedeckt ist, oder ob Pace
der falsche Proxy ist und Schrittlänge / Kadenz / Gefälle-Prozent die
tragenden Größen sind. Zusätzlich: Gilt die aus der Patellofemoral-Literatur
bekannte "+5–10 % Kadenz"-Mitigation auch bergab, ab welchem Gefälle steigt
die exzentrische Last messbar, und lässt sich der Quadrizeps-/Waden-Befund auf
das lumbosakrale Segment übertragen?

## Findings

### F1 — Does per-contact braking load scale with pace such that a pace threshold makes sense? **No.**

Two studies crossed **speed × downhill gradient** with joint-level kinetics.
Both show the opposite of a disproportionate rise.

**Khassetarash et al. 2020** (n = 19, grades 0°/±5°/±10° × speeds 2.50 / 3.33 /
4.17 m·s⁻¹, instrumented treadmill). Energy absorption per stance, summed over
hip + knee + ankle:

| Grade | 2.50 m/s | 3.33 m/s | 4.17 m/s | Δ at +67 % speed |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| −10° | 102.6 J | 121.0 J | 133.8 J | **+30 %** |
| −5° | 77.4 J | 99.8 J | 111.9 J | **+45 %** |

The knee alone at −10° goes 35.7 → 42.8 → **41.7 J** — it *stops rising*.
Normalised per metre travelled, eccentric knee work at −10° actually **falls**
with speed (39.0 → 37.1 → 31.4 J·m⁻¹); the paper reports "12 % lower
normalized energy absorption" at 4.17 vs 2.50 m·s⁻¹ with **no speed × grade
interaction** (P > 0.13). The authors' own mechanism: aerial time saturates on
steep declines, so "energy absorption would not be expected to substantially
increase at faster speeds during steep DR."

**Robinson et al. 2025** (n = 12, −7.5°, three speeds spanning ~14 %): stance-
phase knee negative work **0.622 / 0.606 / 0.629 J·kg⁻¹** — statistically flat
("no significant interaction effects nor main effects of grade were detected
for changes in spatiotemporal or stance phase joint work variables").

**What *does* scale with speed downhill is the loading *rate*, not the work:**
peak negative knee power at −10° rises 10.7 → 14.6 → 18.5 W·kg⁻¹ (+73 % for
+67 % speed), and Vernillo et al. 2020 (Part I, same cohort) report at the
fast/steep corner vs level at matched speed: average normal loading rate
**+52 %**, negative parallel (braking) impulse **+166 %**, peak normal GRF
+9 %. So speed is not irrelevant — but the relationship is smooth and
decelerating in the damage-relevant work term. **There is no kink, therefore
no threshold to place.**

> A caution on secondary sources: the Lu et al. 2025 review attributes
> "quadratic increases in vertical impact peaks … under faster conditions" to
> Gottschall & Kram 2005. That study tested **one speed only (3 m·s⁻¹)** and
> contains zero speed information. Likewise, a "> 40 % higher cumulative damage
> at 5 vs 3 m·s⁻¹ on −10 % slopes" figure circulating from the same review is
> not present in Van Hooren 2024 — that dataset never crossed speed with
> downhill gradient. Neither claim may be cited as data.

### F2 — Why pace is the wrong proxy, and what the right one is

**Pace is confounded.** At an *identical* downhill speed on −6°, Vincent et al.
2019 (n = 40) drove cadence to ±5 % and ±10 % of preferred. Step length moved
from 1.08 m (−10 % cadence) to 0.92 m (+10 % cadence) — a **17 % spread at the
same pace** — and vertical impulse differed by **11.3–14.5 %**. Two runners
descending at the same watch-pace can therefore be in materially different
per-contact load states. Pace cannot see this.

**The mechanically load-bearing per-contact variable is where the foot lands
relative to the body, and step length is its proxy.** Lieberman et al. 2015
(n = 14) tested foot-landing position and stride frequency as competing
predictors of braking force: foot position relative to the hip β = **0.89**
(P = 0.0005) vs stride frequency β = **−0.591**; foot position was the stronger
*direct* predictor, with stride frequency acting through it (5 strides·min⁻¹
higher frequency → 5.9 % shorter landing distance).

**Downhill-specific confirmation that step length is the operative lever:**
Baggaley et al. 2020 (n = 19, 3.33 m·s⁻¹, step length ±10 % × five grades)
found the knee most responsive to step length, and the effect *largest on
declines*: "Shortening step length reduced knee joint energy absorption at all
grades with the smallest effect observed during uphill running (Δ ≥ −0.053
J/kg), while large reductions occurred during level (Δ = −0.096 J/kg) and
downhill running (Δ ≥ −0.108 J/kg)."

**And the default behaviour runs the wrong way.** When runners speed up
downhill they lengthen the step, they do not lift cadence:

| Study | Grade | Step length | Cadence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Robinson 2025 (n = 12) | −7.5° | 1.17 → 1.24 → 1.29 m | 168 → 168 → 173 spm |
| Sundström 2021 (n = 6) | −5° | 2.66 → 3.33 m (stride) | 75.6 → 79.5 min⁻¹ |
| Sundström 2021 | −10° | 2.66 → **3.68 m** | 75.6 → **72.2** min⁻¹ (speed effect on SF: p = 0.755, n.s.) |

Cadence-lifting is the *uphill* strategy. Going faster downhill is, by default,
step lengthening — exactly the lever Baggaley shows loads the knee. This is the
mechanistic justification for the qualitative rule "faster downhill via
frequency, never via stride length": it is a deliberate override of the
spontaneous strategy.

**What a runner can actually read in real time.** Garmin running-dynamics data
fields displayable live during a run: **cadence (spm)**, **stride length (m)**,
ground contact time (ms), GCT balance, vertical oscillation, vertical ratio
(Garmin owner's manuals, Forerunner 255/265/955/965, fēnix 7, Running Dynamics
Pod). Braking impulse, joint work and foot-landing distance are **not**
available on any consumer device; the IMU research that estimates overstriding
(Kettlewell/Sibson-class three-IMU setups explaining 95–98 % of overstride
variance) is laboratory-validated only and its authors flag drift and
placement as unresolved for field use. **Operative consequence: stride length
is the only load-relevant per-contact variable a watch shows live**, with
cadence as its inverse-coupled companion at fixed pace.

### F3 — Does the "+5–10 % cadence" mitigation transfer to downhill?

**Directionally yes; magnitude at joint level is comparable; but the useful
band is narrower than the level-running literature suggests, and the benefit
does not show up in whole-body GRF metrics.**

- **Modelled tissue level, downhill included** — Van Hooren et al. 2024
  (n = 19): cadence −10 → +10 steps·min⁻¹ at 3.33 m·s⁻¹ reduced cumulative
  weighted damage for patellofemoral joint 11.9 → 11.0 (−7.6 %), tibia
  125 → 117 (−6.4 %), Achilles 7.72 → 6.90 (−10.6 %), all p < 0.001. Note this
  is **per kilometre** — it already nets out the higher step count.
- **Measured joint work on a decline** — Baggaley 2020, above: −10 % step
  length → ≥ −0.108 J·kg⁻¹ knee energy absorption, the largest of any grade.
- **Measured whole-body kinetics on a decline** — Vincent 2019 is the important
  counterweight: at −6°, cadence manipulation "affected aspects of K_vert and
  vertical impulses but **not GRF or rate of loading**". Peak GRF moved 0–4.3 %
  (n.s.), VALR n.s. Only vertical impulse responded, and only at the slow end
  (−10 % cadence: +11.3–14.5 % vs the other conditions). Vertical stiffness
  rose +16.5 % at +10 % cadence.
- **Cost of forcing it** — Vincent 2019: caloric unit cost was minimised at
  preferred and ±5 %, and rose at ±10 %; RPE was elevated in **every** cadence
  condition relative to level running, and the authors conclude "changing
  cadence across level and downhill stretches is likely not necessary and may
  actually increase perceived effort", with the operative band being
  "cadences that range +/-5 % of preferred".

**Synthesis:** the level-running rehab dose (+5–10 % from self-selected,
Heiderscheit 2011 — see [running-cadence-myths.md](running-cadence-myths.md))
transfers downhill in direction and rough magnitude at the joint, but **+5 % is
the defensible operating point on a descent, not +10 %**, and the athlete
should expect the change to cost RPE without showing up in impact-force
metrics. Cadence is the *mediator*; step length is the *lever* (F2). Prescribe
the short step and let cadence follow, rather than metronoming cadence.

### F4 — Gradient as the dose variable: is −2 % justified?

**No. The −2 % mark is a terrain-classification convention, not a load
threshold — no study has ever tested it.** What the literature does support:

- **Measurable knee-load elevation is documented from about −5 %.** Van Hooren
  2024 found significantly increased patellofemoral peak stress and cumulative
  damage already at **−3° (≈ −5.2 %)** vs level, with a clean per-degree dose:
  peak patellofemoral stress "increases by 0.57 MPa with every degree increase
  in downhill gradient at 2.78 m s⁻¹". Cumulative patellofemoral weighted
  impulse: +6° 9.05 | +3° 10.2 | 0° 11.5 | **−3° 13.2** | **−6° 15.5**
  (increments +1.7 then +2.3 — mildly convex in gradient, not in speed).
  Khassetarash 2020 at −5° (≈ −8.7 %) already shows knee absorption 45 % above
  level (28.4 vs 19.6 J at 2.50 m·s⁻¹).
- **The older −7 % claim is superseded.** Vernillo et al. 2017 inferred "a
  minimal gradient of −7 % is necessary to significantly increase knee power
  absorption" — but from a two-study contrast (a null at ±5 %, a positive at
  −7 %), not a dose-response design. Van Hooren's −3° result post-dates and
  undercuts it.
- **The EIMD/DOMS literature has never tested shallow grades.** Bontemps et al.
  2020 report the protocol median across the downhill-damage literature as
  "−12 % (−15 %; −10 %)". Whether −2 % or −3 % produces measurable muscle
  damage **has not been asked** — absence of evidence, not evidence of absence.
- **The one genuine mechanical transition sits near −15 %.** Minetti et al.
  2002: the ±0.15 gradient band is where external mechanical work is still a
  mixture of positive and negative; beyond it locomotion is purely negative
  work. Metabolic cost falls monotonically to a minimum at −20 % (Cr 1.73 vs
  3.40 J·kg⁻¹·m⁻¹ level) with **no kink anywhere near −2 %**.
- **The knee is not the only tissue, and the tissues disagree.** Downhill
  *reduces* Achilles load (Van Hooren: cumulative Achilles 6.87 at −6° vs 10.1
  at +6°) while raising patellofemoral and tibial load. Khassetarash adds a
  depth-of-grade shift: the knee and hip extensors are the primary absorbers on
  **shallow** declines (−5°), the ankle plantar flexors and hip extensors on
  **steep** ones (−10°) — "the hip and knee extensors, and ankle plantar
  flexors, might be more prone to muscle damage during shallow and steep DR,
  respectively." A gradient cap must therefore be tied to *which* tissue is the
  limiter.

**Defensible operational statement:** treat **−5 %** as the shallowest gradient
with documented knee-load elevation, and the **−10 % to −15 %** band as where
the eccentric character dominates. Anything shallower than −5 % is
undocumented, in either direction.

### F5 — Transfer to the lumbosacral segment: an explicit evidence gap

**The quadriceps/calf eccentric-damage story does NOT transfer to the lumbar
spine on current evidence, and two of the few measured findings run against the
analogy.** This section is deliberately negative — see the caveat rule below.

**What has been measured (thin but real):**

- **Sacral shock.** Mizrahi et al. 2000/2001 (n = 14, 30 min at AT+5 %, −4° ≈
  −7 %, accelerometers at tibial tuberosity and sacrum): "The downhill running
  related with eccentric muscle contractions was associated with increased
  shock propagation from the tibial tuberosity to the sacrum levels, even
  though fatigue did not develop." Larger **peak** sacral acceleration than
  level, but **no difference in impact-frequency content at the sacrum**. The
  abstract carries no g-values and the full text is paywalled — cite the
  direction, never a number.
- **Foot strike modifies sacral shock on a real descent.** Giandolini et al.
  2016 (n = 23, 6.5 km / 1264 m descent, accelerometers incl. sacrum):
  forefoot strike gave "lower transverse peak acceleration at the sacrum" and
  "lower sacral peak acceleration" for the resultant; "the attenuation of axial
  and resultant impact-related vibrations was improved using anterior FSPs".
- **Lumbar posture shifts, slightly.** Levine et al. 2007 (n = 20, 2.9 m·s⁻¹,
  ±5°): mean lumbar sagittal position running level 6.9 ± 4.1°, downhill
  10.0 ± 4.2°; sagittal ROM 10.9 ± 4.3° → 14.5 ± 5.3°. Engeler et al. 2025
  (n = 12, 0 / ±5 / ±10 / ±15 %): lumbar lordosis increased at −15 % by
  1.2 ± 4.9° (p = 0.007); pelvic tilt 12.3° level → 17.2° at −15 %.

**What is contradicted by measurement:**

- **Erector spinae does not work harder downhill.** Engeler 2025: "The erector
  spinae was the only muscle to exhibit reduced activity, with decreases
  observed at −10 %, −5 %, +10 % and +15 %" (stance mean %MVIC: 55.5 at level
  vs 49.4 at −10 %).
- **The trunk leans backward downhill, not forward.** Engeler 2025 trunk
  inclination: −1.4° at level → **−3.4°** at −15 % (d = −1.17). The
  "forward-leaning trunk downhill" claim in the review literature is a post-hoc
  interpretation of *hip flexion* subgroup differences (Vernillo 2017 on Chu &
  Caldwell 2004), not a measurement of trunk posture on a grade.

**What does not exist at all:**

- **Zero** lumbar kinetics in downhill running — no compression, no shear, no
  L5/S1 reaction force, no disc load, measured or modelled, at any gradient.
- **Zero** studies linking downhill running, descent volume or gradient to low
  back pain. Maselli et al. 2020 (systematic review, 19 studies, runner LBP
  prevalence 0.7–20.2 %) contains no gradient or slope variable at all and
  concludes "running could be interpreted as a protective factor against the
  onset of LBP". The ultra-trail dataset most often invoked for the opposite
  (Malliaropoulos 2015, n = 40, LBP 42.5 %) reports "running in the mountains
  (p = 0.0004)" as a **protective** factor. Wu et al. 2021 (n = 800, marathon)
  did not assess terrain.
- **Zero** cadence- or step-length-manipulation studies with a lumbar load
  outcome. The nearest is Baggaley 2020's peak sacral acceleration, a
  non-significant trend at ±10 % step length (preferred vs +10 % step rate SMD
  −0.63, CI −1.29 to 0.02). Anderson et al. 2022 (systematic review + meta,
  37 step-rate studies) contains no lumbar, downhill or back-pain content and
  states "No data pooling was possible for any trunk or pelvis kinetic
  findings". Nahhas Rodacki et al. 2024 is the only study with a genuine spinal
  load outcome and a cadence exposure (stature loss vs cadence, R² = 0.38) —
  **level running, 10 km·h⁻¹, cadence observed not manipulated**; not
  transferable to gradients.

**Three authoritative downhill-running reviews — Vernillo 2017, Bontemps 2020,
Lu 2025 — contain no lumbar, spine, trunk, pelvis, lordosis, low-back or
erector-spinae content whatsoever.** Any coaching claim that downhill running
is a lumbar-loading stressor by analogy with quadriceps eccentric damage is at
present **speculation without measured lumbar kinetic support**. A downhill cap
prescribed for a lumbosacral limiter must be declared as a *precautionary*
measure, not as an evidence-backed one — and the two mechanistic details that
have been measured (reduced erector spinae activity, backward trunk lean) do
not support the analogy.

## Application in framework

1. **`config.example/training_paradigms.md` → "Trail-specific training" /
   "Intensity blocks".** The downhill-technique bullet currently says "focus on
   short ground-contact time and upright posture". Two corrections are due:
   (a) GCT is *not* the cue with evidence behind it — **step length** is
   (Baggaley 2020); (b) "upright posture" conflicts with the only measured
   trunk data on declines (Engeler 2025: trunk shifts backward, lumbar lordosis
   increases). Reword to a step-length cue plus a gradient dose reference to
   this document.
2. **A downhill cap belongs in a `Gefälle-Deckelung` rule, and it is
   step-length-anchored, not pace-anchored.** Operative form: *"Auf Gefälle
   ≥ −5 %: Schrittlänge nicht über dem eigenen Flach-Z2-Baseline-Wert; bei
   aktivem exzentrischem Limiter ~10 % darunter (Baggaley 2020). Kadenz folgt,
   sie wird nicht metronomisiert; wenn eine Kadenz-Vorgabe nötig ist, +5 % vom
   Selbstgewählten, nicht +10 % (Vincent 2019). Pace ist auf dem Gefälle keine
   Steuergröße."* The athlete-individual baseline stride length lives in
   `config/athlete_status.md`, never here.
3. **`agents/specialist-endurance.md`.** This is the same discipline the agent
   already applies to strides ("Judge stride execution by dynamics, not GPS
   pace"). Extend that principle explicitly to descents: on a downhill segment
   the steering variables are **gradient + stride length + cadence**, and pace
   is a result, not a target. Any downhill instruction written into an
   `intervals_icu` step should carry a stride-length/cadence cue, never an
   `X s/km` corridor.
4. **Retire the "max ~X s/km under section pace" heuristic.** It has no
   literature coverage, and the mechanism it assumes (a disproportionate rise
   in braking load above a pace deviation) is contradicted by the only two
   speed × downhill-gradient datasets. Removing it is a correction, not a
   loosening — the step-length rule that replaces it is *more* restrictive on
   the variable that actually carries the load.
5. **Gradient thresholds where a number is used at all:** −5 % as the shallowest
   documented knee-load elevation, −10 % to −15 % as the eccentric-dominant
   band. A −2 % or −3 % figure must not be presented as evidence-based.
6. **Lumbosacral limiters:** a downhill cap prescribed for a low-back limiter
   is **precautionary**, and coach communication must say so. Do not cite
   "eccentric downhill load" as the mechanism — that chain is unmeasured at the
   lumbar spine (F5). The one measured mitigation with a sacral outcome on a
   real descent is foot-strike-related (Giandolini 2016), and its effect at the
   sacrum is modest.
7. **Cross-references:** builds on
   [downhill-running-doms-taper.md](downhill-running-doms-taper.md) (EIMD
   timeline, taper rule — unchanged and not contradicted; this document adds
   the *within-run* dosing layer it lacks),
   [doms-peak-timing.md](doms-peak-timing.md) (slow-eccentric signature and the
   ≥ 72 h spacing floor that downhill running falls under),
   [running-cadence-myths.md](running-cadence-myths.md) (the +5 % dose origin
   and the "self-selected ≈ optimal" caveat, which Vincent 2019 now confirms
   *on declines* as well),
   [running-posture-pelvic-tilt-and-hip-extension.md](running-posture-pelvic-tilt-and-hip-extension.md)
   (F5's lordosis/pelvic-tilt findings extend it to graded running),
   [strava-vs-intervals-gap.md](strava-vs-intervals-gap.md) (GAP models rest on
   the Minetti cost curve, which says nothing about tissue load — a reminder
   that grade-adjusted *pace* is not grade-adjusted *load*).

**Contradiction flagged:** none against existing framework documents.
`downhill-running-doms-taper.md` claim 3 ("race-day pacing for downhill should
be more conservative than uphill") survives, but its *justification* must
change: the reason is cumulative eccentric exposure over race duration, **not**
a per-contact load that explodes above some downhill speed.

## Open questions / Caveats

1. **No study has crossed speed × downhill gradient with a tissue-level damage
   model.** Khassetarash/Robinson give joint work and power; Van Hooren gives
   modelled tissue damage but tested gradient only at one speed (2.78 m·s⁻¹).
   Because Van Hooren's damage proxy raises peak stress to a fatigue-life
   exponent of 7 (bone) / 9.3 (tendon), a modest per-contact peak-stress rise
   converts into a large damage estimate — so a **tissue-level** speed penalty
   on descents remains theoretically possible even though the **work-level**
   one is sub-proportional. This is the single most useful missing experiment
   for this question.
2. **Everything above is treadmill work except Giandolini 2016.** Real descents
   add surface irregularity, braking for corners, visual/attentional load and
   fear-modulated landing stiffness — Vincent 2019 explicitly speculates that
   "landing patterns were tentative and soft due to fear of falling" explains
   their null GRF result. Field transfer is untested.
3. **The step-length rule has no validated absolute number.** Baggaley's
   −10 % is a manipulation magnitude, not a prescription threshold, and it was
   tested at one speed (3.33 m·s⁻¹). "10 % below the athlete's flat baseline"
   is a defensible operationalisation of a real effect, **not** a validated
   dose.
4. **Individual strategy varies more than the group means suggest.** Robinson
   2025 documents one subject who was purely step-length-dominant and another
   purely step-frequency-dominant across all grades — "not all runners adhere
   to the commonly accepted mechanical strategies for increasing speed". The
   athlete's own descent baseline should be measured, not assumed.
5. **Gradient below −5 % is genuinely unknown**, in both directions. A coach may
   not claim shallow declines are safe *or* that they are loading.
6. **No downhill EIMD study has used a shallow grade**, so the whole
   damage-timeline literature (which
   [downhill-running-doms-taper.md](downhill-running-doms-taper.md) rests on)
   describes −10 % to −20 % exposures. Applying its 4–7 day recovery windows to
   a rolling −4 % course is an extrapolation.
7. **The lumbar gap is the largest one here** and unlikely to close soon: no
   lumbar kinetics in downhill running exist at all, and no study has tested
   whether descent exposure predicts low back pain in runners. Re-check this
   sub-question in 12–24 months; Engeler 2025 is the first laboratory study to
   look at spinal posture across gradients and may seed follow-ups.
8. **Fatigue was not a variable in any of the speed × gradient work.**
   Mizrahi's shock-propagation finding held "even though fatigue did not
   develop" over 30 min; how a 2–4 h descent-heavy effort changes the picture
   is unaddressed by the studies cited here.
