# Race-surface exposure in the recurring easy volume — is there a dose?

**Erstellt:** 2026-09-01

## TL;DR

**No. No study has tested how much running volume on a target surface is
required to build tolerance to that surface, and a substantial part of the
assumed surface effect is cancelled by the runner inside a single step.**
Three statements survive the evidence check. (1) Surface changes the
*distribution* of load far more than its magnitude: in a controlled impact
experiment, interface hardness explained **< 10 %** of the variance in tibial
acceleration and impact force, against **25–29 %** for knee angle and
**35–48 %** for muscle pre-activation (Potthast 2010); runners re-tune leg
stiffness **on the first step** after a surface transition (Ferris 1999), so
support mechanics stay essentially unchanged across a 12.5-fold surface-
stiffness range (Kerdok 2002). Field data are correspondingly inconsistent —
tibial acceleration is lower on woodchip than concrete (Boey 2017), *higher*
on grass than concrete at level grade (Waite 2021), and not different at all
between dirt, gravel and paved trail surfaces (Garcia 2021). (2) The tissue-
adaptation literature has a dose — but the dose variable is **load magnitude
and its novelty**, not surface identity: tendon stiffness adaptation scales
with loading *intensity* over ≥ 8–12 weeks and not with contraction type
(Bohm 2015); bone responds to *dynamic* loading and its cells **accommodate to
the customary loading environment** (Turner 1998). Surface appears as a
variable in neither. (3) Where the evidence is explicit, it points against the
usual assumption, not for it: surface has never reached the strong-evidence
tier in any running-injury review (van Gent 2007), was pre-specified in a
prospective cohort and dropped out of every model (Taunton 2003), and a 2024
scoping review states plainly that the soft-surface recommendation is
**"unfounded"** (George 2024) — while the same authors warn that the null may
be an artefact of exposure measurement too crude to detect a real effect. The
tendinopathy guidelines carry no surface recommendation at any grade, and
surface is not among the risk factors that survive review (van der Vlist 2019).
(4) **The two sides of the conflict turn out to be one finding read from
opposite ends.** Compliant and/or uneven ground gives the calf–Achilles unit
*less* work per step than flat firm ground — plantarflexor negative work
roughly halves on compliant substrate (Grant 2022) and ankle work falls 18–22 %
on uneven terrain (Voloshina 2015). From the rehab end that is an offload; from
the race end it is exactly the demand the athlete is not accumulating.

**Operative rule: name the race-surface share as a coaching *decision*, never as an
evidence-backed dose; keep the exposure non-zero and recurring rather than
concentrated in a handful of quality sessions; progress it as a novel load
(graded, one variable at a time); and audit it with the 24-hour tissue
response, which is the only monitoring criterion the literature actually
supports.**

## Question / Trigger

Auslöser: coach-geflaggte Unsicherheit aus realer Anwendung. Generischer Fall:
Ein Läufer bereitet ein Rennen auf einer Oberflächen-Klasse vor (typisch: hart
und eben), während das wiederkehrende Easy-/Grundlagen-Volumen auf einer
anderen läuft (typisch: nachgiebig und uneben) — häufig, weil ein
Gewebeschutz-Protokoll die nachgiebige Oberfläche empfiehlt. Der Plan bucht die
Renn-Oberfläche dann nur in wenige Quality-Einheiten. Der Coach muss den
Konflikt entscheiden, ohne ein Verhältnis zu behaupten, das er nicht belegen
kann.

Leitfrage: **Wie viel des Easy-Volumens muss auf der Renn-Oberfläche laufen, um
oberflächenspezifische Toleranz aufzubauen — und existiert überhaupt
Dosis-Wirkungs-Evidenz für oberflächenspezifische Gewebeadaptation, oder
überträgt sich allgemeine Laufbelastung über Oberflächen hinweg?**

Teilfragen:

1. Verändert die Oberfläche die Gewebebelastung überhaupt materiell — und wo
   gleicht der Läufer sie selbst wieder aus?
2. Gibt es Dosis-Wirkungs-Evidenz für oberflächenspezifische Adaptation
   (Knochen, Sehne, Plantarfaszie, Sprunggelenks-Stabilisatoren)?
3. Was sagt die Verletzungs-Epidemiologie zur Oberfläche als Risikofaktor — und
   wie gut ist diese Evidenz?
4. Oberflächen-**Variabilität** vs. Oberflächen-**Spezifität**: senkt Wechseln
   das Risiko?
5. Wie interagiert das mit einem Reha-Protokoll, das eine nachgiebige
   Oberfläche empfiehlt — ist diese Empfehlung selbst belegt?
6. Praktische Übersetzung, nur so weit die Evidenz trägt.

## Findings

### F1 — The surface effect is real at the interface and largely cancelled by the runner

The classical mechanism is well established and points in an uncomfortable
direction for surface prescriptions: the runner is not a passive receiver of
the surface.

- **Leg stiffness is the compensating variable.** Ferris, Louie & Farley (1998)
  showed that "human runners adjust their leg stiffness to accommodate changes
  in surface stiffness, allowing them to maintain similar running mechanics on
  different surfaces."
- **The adjustment is immediate, not trained.** Ferris, Liang & Farley (1999)
  measured the transition itself: "runners completely adjusted leg stiffness
  for their first step on the new surface after the transition" — a 29 % leg-
  stiffness change between the last soft step and the first hard step, with
  centre-of-mass vertical displacement during stance unchanged (~7 cm) despite
  surface compression falling from 6 cm to < 0.25 cm.
- **The compensation holds across a wide stiffness range.** Kerdok et al.
  (2002) ran subjects over five platform stiffnesses spanning a 12.5-fold
  range: "The 12.5-fold decrease in surface stiffness resulted in a 12 %
  decrease in the runner's metabolic rate and a 29 % increase in their leg
  stiffness. The runner's support mechanics remained essentially unchanged."

**This is the single most important finding for the planning question, and it
cuts against the intuition on both sides.** If the neuromuscular accommodation
to a surface is complete within one step, there is no *neuromuscular* tolerance
to build over weeks — the athlete already has it. Whatever surface-specific
tolerance is worth training must therefore live in the slow tissues (bone,
tendon, plantar fascia), which is exactly where the dose evidence is missing
(F3).

The relative weight of surface against the runner's own strategy has been
quantified once, directly. Potthast et al. (2010) drove standardised external
impacts under the heel while manipulating interface hardness, knee angle and
muscle pre-activation:

> "The hardness of the two shock interfaces explained less than 10 % of the
> variance of accelerations and impact forces, whereas knee angle changes
> explained 25–29 %, and preactivation changes explained 35–48 % of the
> variances."

Field measurements match that picture — they disagree with each other, which is
itself the finding:

| Study | Comparison | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Dixon 2000 (n = 6, shod) | asphalt vs rubber-modified asphalt vs acrylic | **No significant difference in peak impact force**; loading rate lower on rubber-modified asphalt; individual kinematic strategies varied |
| Boey 2017 (n = 35) | concrete vs synthetic track vs woodchip trail | Tibial vertical acceleration **lower on woodchip** than track and concrete |
| Waite 2021 (n = trained distance runners) | grass vs asphalt vs concrete × grade | Peak tibial acceleration **higher on grass** than concrete at level grade (8.22 g vs 7.47 g) and higher than asphalt on incline |
| Garcia 2021 (n = 15) | dirt vs gravel vs paved, in a trail environment | "**No significant differences** were found for tibial accelerations or shock attenuations among surface types" |
| Tessutti 2010 / 2012 (n = 44 / 47) | asphalt, concrete, rubber, natural grass | Grass lowered peak plantar pressure by **9.3–16.6 %** (rearfoot) and 4.7–12.3 % (forefoot); **asphalt and concrete were statistically similar on every plantar variable** |

Two things follow. First, "soft = less load" is not a general truth; it is
surface-, tissue- and measurement-specific, and reverses in at least one
controlled comparison (grass). Second, the effect that does survive most
reliably is at the **foot–ground interface** (plantar pressure), not at the
tibia — i.e. surface differences are a *plantar/foot* dose more than a
whole-limb dose.

### F2 — Compliance and evenness are two independent axes; conflating them is the common error

A compliant forest path differs from a road on **two** variables, and the
literature treats them separately.

**Compliance** in the laboratory sense means an *elastic, energy-returning*
surface. Kerdok's compliant platforms **reduced** metabolic cost by 12 %, and
the authors attribute this to "an increased energy rebound from the compliant
surfaces studied". A damped, energy-absorbing surface does the opposite — soft
dry sand costs roughly 1.6× the energy of a hard surface at matched speed
(Lejeune 1998, persisted in [sand-running.md](sand-running.md)). **"Soft" is
not one category.** A springy track, a firm forest path, a woodchip trail and
deep sand sit at four different points, and the first is closer to the road
than to the last. Zhou et al. (2021) make the point sharply in the running
case: in habitual non-rearfoot strikers, "GRF peaks were significantly greater
when running on synthetic rubber or artificial grass compared to concrete" —
i.e. two of the "soft" surfaces loaded *more* than the hard one.

**A correction to the usual mechanism.** The intuitive account of why damped
ground costs more — that the elastic recoil the Achilles normally supplies for
free must instead be paid by the calf — has been tested at the muscle level and
is **not supported** (see F6): the extra work appears at the hip and knee,
while plantarflexor work goes *down*. The higher metabolic cost of sand is
real; attributing it to the triceps surae is not.

**Evenness** is a separate demand with its own signature. Voloshina & Ferris
(2015) ran subjects on an uneven-terrain treadmill (≤ 2.5 cm height variation)
against smooth terrain:

- energy expenditure **+5 %**;
- leg stiffness **+20 %** (the same direction as running on a *soft* surface);
- step width and length variability **+27 % / +26 %**;
- vastus medialis / rectus femoris / medial hamstring activity **+7 % / +20 % /
  +19 %**, with increased co-contraction;
- **positive ankle work −22 %, negative ankle work −18 %**, while knee and hip
  work were largely unchanged.

That last line matters for the planning question more than any of the others.
On uneven ground the ankle does **less** positive and negative work per step
while the thigh muscles work harder and co-contract more, and step placement
becomes markedly more variable. A block whose recurring easy volume sits
entirely on compliant uneven ground is therefore not merely "the same running
on a softer floor" — on the measured variables it accumulates **less
plantarflexor work per step** than the same volume on flat even ground, and
more proximal work and step-placement variability. That is a genuine
specificity argument for a flat hard-surface race, and it is a *mechanistic*
one, not a dose-response one. Its limits belong with it: a treadmill with
≤ 2.5 cm height variation is not a forest path, mediolateral joint work was not
reported, and no study has carried this per-step difference through to a
per-block tissue outcome.

### F3 — No dose-response evidence for surface-specific adaptation exists

**This is the direct answer to the flagged question, and it is a null.** No
study was found that trained one group on surface A and another on surface B
and measured tolerance *to* surface A. Searched: PubMed/eutils and Europe PMC
for surface × adaptation / specificity / tolerance / habituation / dose-response
combinations, the surface sections of the running-injury reviews below, and the
tendon- and bone-adaptation literature. What exists is (a) acute biomechanics
(F1, F2), (b) epidemiology of surface as a *risk factor* (F4), and (c) sand
training as a *conditioning modality* — none of which is a surface-tolerance
dose.

What the adaptation literature does have a dose for is instructive, because
surface is not among its variables:

- **Tendon.** Bohm, Mersmann & Arampatzis (2015), meta-analysing 37
  interventions on Achilles and patellar tendon: "the data strongly suggests
  that loading magnitude in particular plays a key role for tendon adaptation
  in contrast to muscle contraction type." Inclusion required interventions
  **≥ 8 weeks**, and effect sizes were higher for **≥ 12 weeks**. The dose
  variables are strain magnitude and duration.
- **Bone.** Turner (1998) states three rules: adaptation "is driven by dynamic,
  rather than static, loading"; "only a short duration of mechanical loading is
  necessary to initiate an adaptive response"; and "bone cells accommodate to a
  customary mechanical loading environment, making them less responsive to
  routine loading signals."

Turner's third rule is the closest thing in the literature to a mechanistic
justification for surface exposure — but read carefully it argues for
**novelty of the strain signature**, not for surface identity. A surface only
matters to bone insofar as it changes strain magnitude or rate, and F1 shows
that it does so weakly and inconsistently, because the runner's knee angle and
pre-activation dominate.

The one measurement that puts real numbers on the bone side is Milgrom et al.
(2003), who instrumented the tibia with a strain-gauged bone staple (**n = 3**)
and compared treadmill against overground running at 11 km/h: axial compression
and tension strains and strain rates were **48–285 % higher overground**, and
the authors conclude that "treadmill runners are at lower risk of developing
tibial stress fractures, but less likely to achieve tibial bone strengthening,
than overground runners." That is the strongest available support for
"exposure to the harder loading environment builds tolerance to it" — and its
limits must be stated with it: n = 3, treadmill-vs-overground (not
hard-vs-soft ground), an inference about outcome from strain rather than a
measured injury outcome, and a comparison confounded by belt mechanics.
It is a mechanistic plausibility argument, **not** a dose.

### F4 — Surface is not an established injury risk factor, and the null may be a measurement artefact

The epidemiological answer is consistent across four decades and is a null:

- **van Mechelen (1992)**, reviewing the epidemiological literature, places
  surface explicitly in the not-associated bucket: "Significantly not
  associated with running injuries seem age, gender, body mass index, running
  hills, **running on hard surfaces**, participation in other sports, time of
  the year and time of the day."
- **Taunton et al. (2003)** is the strongest test available, because surface
  was a **pre-specified** predictor in a prospective cohort — and it dropped
  out: "Factors such as previous activity, arch height, cross training, and
  **running surface were not significant for either sex in any model**."
- **van Gent et al. (2007)** found "strong evidence that a long training
  distance per week in male runners and a history of previous injuries were
  risk factors" — surface reaches no evidence level at all.
- **Saragiotto et al. (2014)**, 4 671 pooled participants and 60 candidate
  factors: the main risk factor is previous injury. Surface is not named.
- **Hulme et al. (2017)**: "The varied effect directions and/or a number of
  statistically insignificant results associated with the majority of factors
  hindered our ability to draw any definitive conclusions."
- The two **meta-analyses** on the injuries the hard-surface folk theory is
  usually invoked for — MTSS (Newman 2013) and lower-limb stress fracture
  (Wright 2015) — could not pool surface at all; it is absent from their
  variable sets. Wright: "only previous history of stress fracture and female
  sex are risk factors … strongly supported by the data."

The two positive findings both come from designs that cannot separate cause
from consequence. van der Worp et al. (2015) report that "running on a concrete
surface" was among factors "associated with a greater risk of injury **in women
than in men**" — a *sex-ratio* statement, not a hardness effect, and the
authors' own conclusion names only previous injury and orthotics. Mousavi et
al. (2021) report hard-surface running at **OR 1.37 (CI 1.17–1.59)** from 804
self-reported questionnaires — where an injured runner who *moved onto* a
different surface produces exactly this association without any causal path.

**The null must be read with its own caveat, and this is the honest centre of
the epidemiology.** Taunton's authors say why their model may have missed a
real effect: "The apparent lack of effect of training surface may stem from the
difficulty of adequately quantifying the time and intensity of running spent on
each of the running surfaces." Surface exposure is measured by asking runners
what they mostly run on. In Taunton's own cohort 69.1 % answered "roads" and
12.3 % answered grass/track/treadmill combined — an exposure variable that
crude cannot resolve a moderate effect. Nielsen et al. (2012) put the same
verdict on the surrounding training-error literature: mean methodological
quality 44.1 %, and "It was not possible to identify which training errors were
related to running related injuries."

So the defensible statement is **not** "surface does not matter". It is:
**surface has never been shown to matter, in a literature whose measurement of
surface is too crude to have shown it.** A coach may therefore not cite
epidemiology *for* a surface ratio — and equally may not cite it as proof that
the choice is free.

**Trail versus road shifts the injury *mix*, not the injury *rate*.** Viljoen
et al. (2021) found the trail injury profile dominated by "skin
lacerations/abrasions … skin blisters, muscle strains, muscle cramping, and
ligament sprains"; Vincent et al. (2022) summarise it as "More than 70 % are
due to overuse, and ankle sprains are the most common acute injury." Uneven
terrain trades a slice of repetitive load for an acute-event exposure. For an
athlete with an inversion history those two are **not commensurable**, and the
framework must not present the swap as a net.

One finding runs the *other* way and deserves naming because it is the only
epidemiological signal in the surface literature that supports specificity at
all: Viljoen et al. (2022), living systematic review of trail-running injury
risk factors, list "**training on asphalt**" among the significant extrinsic
factors — i.e. trail racers who prepared on the road were injured more. Graded
weak (abstract only, paywalled full text, unknown adjustment, and confounded
with preparation specificity rather than hardness), but it is the closest thing
in the literature to "prepare on the surface you race on".

### F5 — Surface variation: an explicit null, and the guidance literature contradicts itself

The hypothesis that *varying* surfaces distributes load across tissues and
lowers injury risk is intuitive and, as far as two independent search passes
reach, **untested by any design** — no RCT, cohort, case-control, or systematic
review treats surface variation as a candidate protective exposure. Searched:
Europe PMC full text for surface-variation / varied-terrain / alternating- and
rotating-surface phrasings, and PubMed for surface-variation × injury-prevention
combinations. The nearest hit is acute accelerometry with no injury outcome and
author-described "small" between-surface effects (Ferro-Sánchez 2023).

The same is true of the **change** of surface as an exposure. The only
peer-reviewed text located that names it does so to say the field's focus on
such factors has not paid off (Harper-Hanigan & Gruber 2022: "a singular focus
on running-related factors such as gait, **running surface change**, sudden
change in running volume … make it difficult to identify reliable mechanisms of
load-related running injury"). Even the far better-studied *distance*-change
rule is thinner than its reputation: Nielsen et al. (2014) found the hazard
ratio for a weekly-distance progression above 30 % at **1.59 (95 % CI
0.96–2.66, P = .07)**, and prefaced it with "It is widely accepted that a sudden increase in running
distance is strongly related to injury in runners. But the scientific knowledge
supporting this assumption is limited."

That the variation question is convention rather than evidence shows most
plainly inside a single review: George et al. (2024) found return-to-running
guidance pointing in opposite directions in the same literature — "Some studies
suggested avoiding multiple terrains during the initial recovery, while others
recommended varying terrain once back to normal training."

**Consequence for planning:** "vary the surface" is a defensible *heuristic*
and an indefensible *rule*. It must not be used as an argument that outranks a
named, decided ratio — but neither may a race-specificity argument be used to
claim that moving all easy volume onto one surface is risk-neutral.

### F6 — The compliant-surface recommendation is convention; one review calls it unfounded

This is the finding that most changes the shape of the coaching decision,
because it removes the asymmetry the coach assumed: the trade is **not** a
documented recommendation against an assumed benefit. Both sides are
conventions.

George, Sheerin & Reid (2024), scoping review of 50 studies on return to
running after tibial bone stress injury (recommendations rated **level IV
evidence** — 39 of 50 included papers were reviews or clinical commentaries):

> "There is a lack of evidence for running on softer surfaces, which is a
> common presumption and recommendation; therefore, at this stage, this
> recommendation is unfounded."

and, on the mechanism:

> "The relationship between surface hardness and injury risk is complex as some
> runners compensate for different running surfaces by altering leg stiffness
> depending on the surface compliance."

The same review's recommendation table shows the conflict openly: "Start on
level asphalt", "Start on running track", "Track may increase strain", "Start
on treadmill", "Start on moderate firmness surface", "Hard surfaces risk
factor/avoid", "Start on softer surfaces initially", "Irregular/uneven/soft
surfaces can increase strain/risk". The guidance base is not a consensus that a
coach can lean on.

For the **tendon** case the check was run separately, and it returns the same
verdict three times over:

- **Guidelines.** Full-text checks of the relevant lower-limb tendinopathy and
  heel-pain clinical practice guidelines find **running surface absent as a
  recommendation at any evidence grade**. The only near-miss is the heel-pain
  CPG's grade-B statement on *work-related weight-bearing activities*
  "particularly under conditions with poor shock absorption" — occupational
  standing on hard floors, resting on low-quality cross-sectional evidence.
  That is not a running-surface prescription and must not be cited as one.
- **Risk-factor literature.** van der Vlist et al. (2019) reviewed clinical
  risk factors for Achilles tendinopathy: 10 cohort studies, "all with a high
  risk of bias", limited evidence for nine factors — **surface is not among
  them** — and "Twenty-six other putative risk factors were not associated with
  Achilles tendinopathy."
- **Intervention trials.** None exist. No trial has tested whether a softer
  training surface improves outcome or lowers recurrence in lower-limb
  tendinopathy.

**And the counter-claim is no better supported than the convention.** The one
epidemiological signal points *away* from soft ground: Knobloch, Yoon & Vogt
(2008), 291 masters runners over 9.98 million km, found "An asphalt running
surface decreased mid-portion tendinopathy risk (RR 0.47, p = 0.02). In
contrast, sand increased the relative risk for mid-portion Achilles
tendinopathy tenfold (RR 10, CI 1.12 to 92.8, p = 0.01)." A confidence interval
spanning nearly two orders of magnitude, from unadjusted descriptive statistics
in a single unreplicated cohort, is a hypothesis, not a finding.

The *mechanism* usually offered for it does not survive either. **No study has
ever measured Achilles tendon force or plantarflexor moment on a compliant
versus hard surface in running.** The best available modelling says the extra
cost of compliant ground is paid **proximally**, not at the calf — Grant et al.
(2022): "our computer simulations suggest there is no increase in the
mechanical work done by the TA, MG, LG and SOL during mid-stance to push-off on
these compliant substrates compared with the hard floor", with hip and knee
extensor work up and plantarflexor negative work roughly **halved** (0.13 vs
0.25 J kg⁻¹). Grant et al. (2024) on sand found MG and SOL activation
differences "not found to be statistically significant". Both are **walking**
studies, which is their limit — but they are the only direct tests there are,
and they point the opposite way from the folk mechanism.

**The synthesis that matters for planning.** Grant 2022 (compliant ground,
walking: plantarflexor negative work halved) and Voloshina 2015 (uneven ground,
running: positive ankle work −22 %, negative −18 %) converge on one statement
from two different experiments: **compliant and/or uneven ground gives the
calf–Achilles unit less work per step than flat firm ground.** Read from the
rehab end, that is an offload — plausibly what a compliant-surface instruction
is reaching for, even though it was never tested. Read from the race end, it is
precisely the demand the athlete is *not* accumulating while the easy volume
sits there. The two sides of the conflict are not two opposing claims; they are
**one finding read from opposite ends**, and that is the honest way to put the
trade to the athlete.

**What this does not license.** The finding is "the soft-surface rule is not
evidence-based", **not** "ignore the rehab protocol". A named clinical
prescription still governs (see *Never silently drop or replace standing
prescriptions* in `framework/CLAUDE.md`); what changes is that the coach may
put the question back to the athlete and the prescribing clinician as an open
one — and can now say precisely what the compliant surface is and is not doing
— instead of treating it as a fixed constraint the race must bend around.

**Two claims to strike from circulation**, both traced to unverifiable sources
during this search: the "≈ 5× body weight impact spike on hard surfaces" line
attributed to McMahon & Greene 1979 (no fetchable primary text), and the claim
that Pinnington et al. (2005) showed greater gastrocnemius activation on sand
(that paper reports significant EMG increases in hip and knee muscles; calf
electrode placement could not be confirmed). Neither may be used in athlete
communication.

evidence-based", **not** "ignore the rehab protocol". A named clinical
prescription still governs (see *Never silently drop or replace standing

### F7 — What the evidence actually supports operationally

No ratio is derivable. The following four statements are:

1. **Exposure must be non-zero and recurring, and quality sessions alone cannot
   carry it.** This is not a dose claim but a structural one: whatever
   surface-specific adaptation exists lives in slow tissues, and the slow
   tissues' dose variables are magnitude × repetitions × weeks (Bohm 2015,
   Turner 1998). A handful of race-pace sessions is the right dose for pace
   and shoe familiarity; it is by construction the wrong dose for anything
   that adapts over weeks.
2. **The transition is the risk, not the surface.** Ridge et al. (2013) is the
   cleanest available analogue: 19 experienced runners transitioned
   *gradually over 10 weeks* to minimalist shoes, and 10 of 19 showed increased
   foot bone-marrow oedema versus the control group (p = 0.009). A change in
   the loading pattern produces measurable tissue response even when it is
   introduced slowly and even in trained runners. Whatever share of easy volume
   moves onto the race surface should therefore ramp, and the ramp is the
   decision that matters — not the endpoint ratio.
3. **Change one variable at a time.** Surface, race shoe and race pace are
   three separate novel loads that a race block tends to introduce together.
   Carbon-plated race footwear has its own habituation requirement and its own
   plantarflexor/Achilles load signature
   ([carbon-plated-race-shoes-load-and-habituation.md](carbon-plated-race-shoes-load-and-habituation.md));
   stacking a shoe change and a surface change into the same session makes the
   24-hour response uninterpretable.
4. **The monitoring criterion is the tissue response, not a number.** The
   return-to-running literature converges on symptom provocation as the
   governing criterion (George 2024: an "individualised graduated return to
   running programme … progressing running distance ahead of speed and
   intensity, with symptom provocation a key consideration"). The framework
   already carries the operational form of this: loading with symptom up to
   ~5/10 during the session is acceptable while the next morning returns to
   ≤ ~2/10 with no stiffness jump and no week-over-week escalation
   ([end-range-loading-tendon-buildup-rom-vs-load.md](end-range-loading-tendon-buildup-rom-vs-load.md)).
   Applied to surface: the 24-hour response to the *first* and *second*
   race-surface easy run decides whether the share goes up, holds, or comes
   back down. That is the substitute for the ratio the literature cannot
   supply.

**On timing before a race.** No study supports a specific lead time. What can
be said is bounded from both sides: the neuromuscular accommodation needs one
step (F1), so a late start is not a *coordination* problem; but the tissue
adaptations that would justify a surface block need ≥ 8–12 weeks in the tendon
literature (Bohm 2015), so a block shorter than that cannot claim structural
adaptation as its rationale. In a short block the honest rationale for
race-surface exposure is **familiarity and load-pattern verification** —
finding out whether the athlete's tissue tolerates the race's loading pattern,
while there is still time to react — not building tolerance. That reframing
matters: it makes the *early* exposures the informative ones, and it makes the
24-hour response the deliverable.

**Evidence grading used above.** *Strong* — controlled laboratory measurement
replicated across labs (leg-stiffness compensation: Ferris ×2, Kerdok).
*Moderate* — single controlled study with small n and consistent mechanism
(Potthast, Voloshina, Dixon, Tessutti ×2). *Weak / contested* — field
accelerometry, where three studies disagree in direction (Boey, Waite,
Garcia). *Very weak* — n = 3 strain-gauge inference (Milgrom); secondary-sourced
case-control signal (sand/Achilles). *Explicit null* — dose-response for
surface-specific adaptation (F3); surface variation as an exposure (F5).

## Application in framework

- **`framework/CLAUDE.md` → "Race surface is a training demand, not only a
  routing default".** The section's evidence caveat was written before this
  research and understates the case: it says the framework carries no
  dose-response evidence, which is correct, but leaves standing the implication
  that the direction ("surface exposure builds surface tolerance") is
  established. It is not — and one of its two premises is actively contradicted
  (the compliant-surface protective assumption). The section should be updated
  to carry (a) the leg-stiffness compensation as the reason the effect is
  smaller than intuition, (b) the finding that *both* sides of the conflict are
  conventions rather than one convention against one recommendation, and (c) the
  24-hour tissue response as the named monitoring criterion that replaces a
  ratio. Exact wording proposed in the head-coach report accompanying this
  document.
- **`config/training_paradigms.md` (athlete-specific) / `config.example/`.**
  Where a wrapper documents surface defaults per run category, the entry should
  reference this document and record the race-surface share as a *decided
  ratio with a date and a reason*, not as an inherited default. No generic
  ratio belongs in `config.example/` — the framework has no number to ship.
- **`framework/research/sand-running.md` — one correction needed.** That
  document states that sand's energy cost is paid by the triceps surae ("The
  runner has to pay metabolically what the tendon recoil normally pays for
  free. This is the mechanistic anchor for the calf/Achilles overload finding")
  and calls sand "a high-load surface for the Achilles". The energetics are
  right; the **attribution to the calf is not supported** — the only direct
  muscle-level tests put the extra work at hip and knee with plantarflexor work
  falling (Grant 2022, 2024). The Knobloch 2008 association stands as an
  association and now has its primary citation, but it should no longer be
  presented as mechanistically explained. Proposed: keep the risk signal,
  replace the mechanism paragraph, add the Knobloch primary in place of the
  secondary source.

## Open questions / Caveats

- **The central question remains unanswered by the literature, not by this
  search.** No surface-specific training-adaptation study exists to be found. A
  ratio presented to an athlete as evidence-backed would be fabricated.
- **All the acute biomechanics are single-session, healthy-runner, small-n
  laboratory or short-field measurements.** None measured cumulative load over
  a training block, and none followed an injury outcome. Extrapolating from a
  per-step acceleration difference to a per-block tissue outcome is exactly the
  step the literature does not license.
- **The tibial-acceleration literature disagrees with itself in direction**
  (woodchip lower, grass higher, trail surfaces no different). Any statement of
  the form "surface X is gentler" should name the study and the tissue, or not
  be made.
- **Potthast 2010 used externally applied heel impacts, not running.** The
  < 10 % variance figure is a strong illustration of the relative weight of
  interface versus the runner's own strategy, but it is not a running
  measurement and should not be quoted as one.
- **Milgrom 2003 (n = 3) is the load-bearing citation for "harder surface =
  more bone stimulus" and it is thin.** It compares treadmill with overground,
  not soft ground with hard ground, and it infers fracture risk and bone
  strengthening from strain rather than measuring either.
- **The sand/Achilles "10×" figure now has its primary (Knobloch 2008) and the
  primary is weak.** Unadjusted descriptive statistics, a single unreplicated
  cohort, CI 1.12–92.8. It is a directional hypothesis, never a magnitude to
  quote to an athlete.
- **The mechanism behind that signal was checked and does not hold.** No study
  has measured Achilles tendon force on compliant versus hard ground **in
  running**; the only direct muscle-level tests (Grant 2022, 2024) are
  **walking** and put the extra work at hip and knee with plantarflexor work
  falling. The convergence with Voloshina 2015 (running, uneven) makes the
  direction credible but the gait mismatch is a real limitation — a running
  measurement of plantarflexor work across surface compliance is the single
  most valuable missing experiment for this question.
- **The epidemiological null is a null of measurement as much as of effect.**
  Surface exposure in these studies is a single self-reported "what do you
  mostly run on" answer. A true moderate effect would not survive that
  instrument. Read the null as "no evidence for", never as "evidence against".
- **The one specificity-supporting epidemiological signal (Viljoen 2022,
  "training on asphalt" as a trail-injury risk factor) was verified from the
  abstract only.** Effect size, adjustment and the underlying study are behind
  a paywall, and the finding is confounded with preparation specificity rather
  than surface hardness. Do not build a rule on it.
- **Checked and negative:** the lower-limb tendinopathy and heel-pain clinical
  practice guidelines carry no running-surface recommendation at any evidence
  grade (full-text checks across six guideline documents). The only surface-
  adjacent statement is occupational, not athletic. Absence of a recommendation
  is not evidence against one — it means the guidelines never took a position.
- **Next check if the question returns:** whether cumulative per-block plantar
  load (pressure-time integral × steps) differs enough between surface classes
  to matter, since the plantar interface is where the surface effect survives
  most consistently. That is computable from the Tessutti data and a step
  count, and would be the first quantitative bridge between an acute
  measurement and a weekly dose.
