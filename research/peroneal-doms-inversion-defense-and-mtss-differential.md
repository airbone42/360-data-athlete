# Peroneal / lower-leg DOMS — impact on dynamic inversion defense, and the differential against MTSS / chronic exertional compartment syndrome after unsupportive footwear exposure

**Erstellt:** 2026-08-05

## TL;DR

Two questions, one decision class: **acute delayed-onset soreness of the
lower-leg muscles after an unusual exposure (unsupportive footwear,
downhill, first-time barefoot / minimalist)** gates (a) whether a
terrain restriction and a walk-out in the fatigue phase are
evidence-based when acute evertor DOMS is present, and (b) whether a
tempo / race-pace stimulus can be released on the following days.

**On the inversion-defense side (F1):**

- **The reflex latency of the peroneals is too slow to prevent the sprain
  anyway** (peripheral EMG onset ≈ 54 ms, active eversion ≈ 176 ms, vs.
  a 30° inversion event in ~80 ms; Konradsen 1997). Protection is
  primarily **feed-forward** — pre-activation, stiffness pre-selection,
  foot-placement strategy. This is the same anchor the existing
  balance-training doc rests on
  ([balance-static-hold-vs-perturbation-ankle-sprain-prevention.md](balance-static-hold-vs-perturbation-ankle-sprain-prevention.md)).
  It means the causal chain "evertor DOMS → longer reflex latency →
  more sprains" mis-locates the mechanism: even a normal reflex arrives
  too late.
- **Eccentric-induced strength loss of the involved muscle is real and
  time-locked**: MVIC deficit is maximal immediately post-exercise (up
  to ~50 % after unaccustomed eccentric bouts), recovers within ~2 days
  when the deficit is < 20 %, up to 7 days when the deficit was
  ≈ 50 %; soreness peaks at 24–72 h and returns to baseline by day 5–7
  (Cheung 2003; general DOMS timeline in
  [doms-peak-timing.md](doms-peak-timing.md)). No study has measured
  isolated peroneal MVIC after a DOMS-inducing bout, so the size of the
  eversion-torque deficit is generic-EIMD, not peroneal-specific.
- **Fatigue lengthens peroneal electromechanical delay** by
  ~ **10–15 ms** (Flevas 2022, N = 15, concentric fatigue to eversion
  torque < 50 %, p = 0.003). This is a **fatigue** effect, not a
  DOMS-specific effect — but it is the closest quantified analogue we
  have and it moves in the direction the intuition assumes.
- **Peroneal fatigue → ankle joint position sense (JPS)** is **not**
  cleanly impaired. Willems 2006 found no JPS effect after
  isolated peroneal fatigue and concluded that peroneal proprioception
  may be fatigue-resistant or that capsule/ligament receptors carry the
  load. The 2024 systematic review + meta-analysis of lower-limb
  fatigue on repositioning sense (Han et al., BMC Sports Sci) reports
  a small-to-moderate effect on ankle active absolute error
  (SDM 0.44 sagittal, 0.54 horizontal), but explicitly does not
  separate concentric fatigue from eccentric-with-DOMS and captures
  only the acute post-exposure window. **The proprioceptive component
  of the caution is thus mechanistically plausible but quantitatively
  soft.**
- **CAI (chronic ankle instability / recurrent sprain history) subjects
  lose their protective compensation under fatigue.** Non-fatigued CAI
  subjects up-regulate peroneal activation to protect the ankle; under
  a running-based fatigue protocol they exhibit *decreased*
  neuromuscular control pre- and post-landing, stiffer landing
  strategies, and biomechanics associated with sprain injury
  (Jeon 2025, N = 20/20/20 CAI vs. copers vs. controls). Copers
  demonstrate the protective compensation that CAI cannot maintain.
  **This is the strongest single piece of evidence that a
  fatigue-analogue state in a CAI-history athlete meaningfully changes
  landing mechanics on the unstable side.** No study has replaced
  "fatigue" with "peroneal DOMS" specifically.

**On the differential side (F2):**

- **Flip-flop / thong-sandal exposure produces higher tibialis anterior
  activation in terminal swing** (increased dorsiflexion to keep the
  sandal on the foot) but does **not** consistently raise lower-leg
  co-contraction indices (Chen 2018). The classic sore-muscle
  distribution after long flip-flop exposure is anterior-lateral
  (tibialis anterior, extensor digitorum longus, peroneus tertius,
  peroneus longus in the eversion role) — **anatomically different
  from the deep posteromedial group** (tibialis posterior, soleus,
  FDL) that carries MTSS.
- **MTSS diagnosis is defined by clinical criteria the athlete can
  apply themselves**: reproducible tenderness on palpation of a
  diffuse area ≥ 5 cm along the **posteromedial tibial border**
  (i.e. the medial edge of the shin bone itself), exercise-induced
  pain that often eases during warm-up and returns / worsens after,
  worse the next morning, absent cramping/burning/paresthesia
  (StatPearls). The single most useful bedside discriminator is
  **"does pressing along the inner edge of the shin bone reproduce
  the pain?"** — yes ≈ MTSS; no, only muscle-belly tenderness ≈ DOMS.
- **Chronic exertional compartment syndrome (CECS)** presents with
  cramping / squeezing / burning that starts predictably 15–20 min
  into exertion, is often bilateral, and **resolves quickly after
  activity stops** — plus paresthesia / focal weakness in ~1/3 of
  cases. CECS is the most common initial misdiagnosis as "shin
  splints", so it belongs in the differential.
- **Return-to-tempo criteria after a first-time unsupportive-footwear
  exposure (pure DOMS)**: no evidence supports deferring tempo beyond
  the biomechanically compromised window. Practical heuristic derived
  from the DOMS timeline + running-economy literature: **no tempo /
  race-pace stimulus while the peak DOMS window (24–72 h) is active**
  (running economy is impaired at ≥ 85 % VO₂max under EIMD, stride
  mechanics are altered, RPE inflation invalidates the stimulus as a
  clean progression datapoint); tempo release when normal walking is
  pain-free and the shin-bone-palpation test is negative
  (differentiating simple DOMS from a beginning MTSS trajectory). No
  Feld-marker outperforms daily symptom monitoring; imaging is
  not available in this setting.

**Bottom line for the coach team:**

- The concrete conservative default from the flag ("48–72 h no uneven
  terrain during acute evertor DOMS, walk-out in the fatigue phase,
  no race-pace block") is **mechanistically defensible and empirically
  under-tested** — the evidence base supports the *direction*, not the
  *magnitude*. For an athlete with recurrent-sprain history, the
  strongest single indirect anchor is Jeon 2025 (CAI subjects lose
  protective landing mechanics under fatigue-analogue stress). For an
  athlete *without* CAI history the same caution is proportionally
  thinner and can be relaxed sooner (48 h ceiling).
- The primary risk-modifier under the coach's control is **exposure
  reduction** (fewer inversion trigger events per unit time = flat
  ground, walk-out at fatigue, no race pace on trail while acute), not
  peroneal-defense enhancement (DOMS heals on the day-timescale; no
  short-term intervention improves reflex latency). This is consistent
  with the wider ankle-sprain-prevention paradigm: acute protection
  comes from external stützung + terrain choice; neuromuscular
  training builds capacity over weeks, not days
  ([cai-perturbation-training-programming.md](cai-perturbation-training-programming.md)).
- The MTSS vs. simple DOMS differential is athlete-answerable in one
  question — **"press along the inner edge of the shin bone for 5 cm;
  is that painful?"** — and separates a benign recovery pathway
  (simple DOMS, tempo release at 72 h if walking is pain-free) from a
  bone-stress-injury trajectory (MTSS, deferred tempo and a
  progression-cap course per StatPearls: pain-free 2 consecutive days
  → pain-free 30-min walk → 10 %-per-week build).

## Question / Trigger

**Auslöser:** two coach-flagged uncertainties from real application
converging on the same class of decisions —
(1) whether an evidence-based restriction on uneven terrain is
justified while acute evertor DOMS is present, or whether the caution
only reduces exposure frequency without changing per-event risk;
(2) how to differentiate exertional lower-leg soreness after
unsupportive-footwear exposure (flip-flops / thong sandals) from a
beginning MTSS / compartment reaction, and when a tempo / race-pace
stimulus can be released on the following days.

**Generic sport-science framing:**

- **F1:** How much does acute delayed-onset muscle soreness of the
  peroneal / fibular muscles impair the dynamic inversion-defense
  function? Specifically: eversion-strength deficit, peroneal reflex
  latency to a sudden inversion perturbation, ankle joint position
  sense — magnitude and time course over the 24 / 48 / 72 h window.
  And: does a magnitude measured in a healthy sample transfer to a
  population with recurrent lateral ankle sprain (CAI), or is the
  effect amplified there?
- **F2:** Which bedside criteria separate exertional lower-leg
  muscular soreness after unsupportive-footwear exposure (mechanism:
  elevated dorsiflexor + evertor activity, toe-curling to retain the
  shoe) from a beginning medial tibial stress syndrome / chronic
  exertional compartment syndrome? And when is a tempo / race-pace
  stimulus releasable — is there a measurable trajectory marker, or
  is the release necessarily clinical / symptom-guided?

Both questions gate whether the coach team's conservative default
(flat ground while acute, walk-out at fatigue, tempo held back with
tight in-session abort criteria) is supported by evidence or is a
mechanism-only heuristic that should be relaxed.

## Findings

### 1. Peroneal DOMS — eversion strength deficit and time course

**Generic eccentric-EIMD timeline** (from
[doms-peak-timing.md](doms-peak-timing.md), primary anchor Cheung 2003
*Sports Med* 33(2):145–164): asymptomatic 0–12 h; onset 12–24 h; peak
soreness and −20 to −30 % strength loss 24–72 h; myofibrillar repair
starts day 3; complete recovery day 5–7. Muscle-damage-indicated MVIC
studies refine this: the decrease in MVIC torque is **greatest
immediately or 1 day post-exercise**, while soreness peaks 1–3 days
post-exercise; when initial strength loss is ≤ 20 %, full recovery
occurs within ~2 days, when it is ≈ 50 % (typical for a first
unaccustomed exposure) strength stays below baseline through day 7
(Nosaka 2021, *PubMed 34706946*).

**Peroneal-specific data are absent.** No study has quantified the
eversion-torque deficit after a DOMS-inducing peroneal / evertor
protocol. Practically the coach must anchor on the generic
eccentric-EIMD envelope: **assume up to −20–30 % eversion strength at
24–72 h post-exposure and full recovery by day 5–7**, with the caveat
that the exposure that produced this (walking in flip-flops, first
minimalist run, extended toe-curl retention) is typically low-load,
long-duration — closer to the "moderate-deficit → 2 day recovery"
band than to the "50 %-deficit → 7 day recovery" band.

**Implication.** The strength component of the mechanism is real
during the acute window (24–72 h) and largely gone by day 5–7. It is
the size of the deficit *relative to the demand of the corrective
action* that matters — and here the finding in section 2 becomes
decisive: the corrective action of the reflex arrives too late anyway,
so the strength deficit does not translate into a documented
"per-event inversion-defense failure" that the coach can quantify.

### 2. Peroneal reflex latency, electromechanical delay, and why the reflex "does not save the sprain"

**The reflex is too slow at baseline.** Konradsen/Voigt/Højsgaard 1997
(*Am J Sports Med* 25(1):54–58, trapdoor model): 30° inversion
completes in ~80 ms; peroneal EMG onset arrives at ≈ 54 ms; the
electromechanical delay adds ~72 ms; active eversion moment develops
at ~176 ms — well after the ligament injury window. The authors'
verbatim conclusion: *"Both peripheral and central reactions, however,
seem too slow to protect the ankle in case of sudden inversion
occurring at the time of heel contact."* This is the canonical
mechanistic anchor and is already cited in the framework's balance
research
([balance-static-hold-vs-perturbation-ankle-sprain-prevention.md](balance-static-hold-vs-perturbation-ankle-sprain-prevention.md)
§ 2d).

**Baseline peroneal latency in CAI is not uniformly delayed.**
Vaes/Duquet/Van Gheluwe 2002 (*J Athl Train*, N = 48 unstable / 46
healthy) measured peroneal reaction time in a sudden-inversion
platform: healthy 28.4 ms vs. unstable 25.5 ms (p = 0.27, i.e. **no
significant difference**); eversion motor response 80.5 ms vs.
81.5 ms (also not significantly different). The literature is
divided: multiple studies report *delayed* activation of the
peroneus longus in CAI in response to sudden inversion, in
double- to single-limb transitions, and in landing on unstable
surfaces (recent narrative reviews, e.g. Xu 2024, *ScienceDirect*).
The safest reading is that CAI does not uniformly show a longer
resting reflex latency, but does show altered activation timing in
functionally loaded tasks — closer to a **motor-strategy** deficit
than a **reflex-arc** deficit.

**Fatigue lengthens electromechanical delay.** Flevas et al. 2022
(*SICOT-J*, N = 15 healthy amateur athletes) fatigued the evertors
concentrically until eversion torque dropped below 50 % for three
consecutive repetitions, then re-measured peroneal EMD from a
sudden-inversion platform. **Fatigue increased EMD by 10 ms
(dominant leg) and 15 ms (non-dominant leg), p = 0.003.** Authors'
conclusion: *"Fatigued ankles demonstrate longer EMD times, implying
that improving resistance to fatigue may add another layer of
protection that has the potential to prevent ankle sprain
recurrence."* This is **fatigue**, not DOMS, but it is the closest
quantified analogue and moves in the direction the coach's intuition
assumes. **Magnitude relative to the mechanism:** adding 10–15 ms to
a total latency (≈ 176 ms) shifts active protection deeper into a
window that was already too late — the effect is real but does not
convert a "protected" into an "unprotected" event; both are
unprotected on the pure-reflex time budget.

**Confidence for F1 on this axis.** *Quantitatively belegt* on
fatigue, *mechanistically extrapolated* on DOMS, and importantly
**the pure-reflex axis is not where the protection lives anyway** —
feed-forward pre-activation is (Konradsen 1997, Hayek 2023 on
anticipatory activation in
[balance-static-hold-vs-perturbation-ankle-sprain-prevention.md](balance-static-hold-vs-perturbation-ankle-sprain-prevention.md)
§ 2e). Whether feed-forward pre-activation is impaired by DOMS is
open (see Caveat 1).

### 3. Peroneal DOMS and joint position sense — mixed evidence, small effect

**Isolated peroneal-fatigue → JPS: null.** Willems et al. 2006
(*Phys Ther Sport* 7(4):176–184, referenced via
[the peroneal-fatigue JPS abstract](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1466853X06001532))
fatigued the peroneal muscles selectively and re-measured
inversion / eversion active joint position sense. **No significant
JPS change.** Authors' interpretation: peroneal proprioception may be
fatigue-resistant, or capsule / ligament mechanoreceptors carry the
proprioceptive load rather than the muscle spindles of the fatigued
muscle. A later study by Lin et al. 2008 (*Isokinet Exerc Sci*
16:181–187) found a small decrement in *passive* inversion JPS after
evertor fatigue — the effect is neither zero nor large.

**Whole-limb lower-extremity fatigue → JPS: small-to-moderate,
acute-only.** Han et al. 2024 (*BMC Sports Sci Med Rehabil*,
[PMC10840207](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10840207/),
systematic review and meta-analysis of fatigue on lower-limb
repositioning sense) report significant effects on **active absolute
angular error** at the ankle: SDM 0.443 (95 % CI 0.088–0.798) sagittal
plane and SDM 0.541 (95 % CI 0.367–0.715) horizontal plane; **passive**
angular error at the ankle was not significantly affected (SDM 0.362,
95 % CI −0.045–0.769). Authors note that included studies "lacked a
follow-up assessment", so the review captures only the acute
post-fatigue window and does not distinguish concentric-only from
eccentric-with-DOMS.

**Eccentric-exercise-with-DOMS → proprioception more broadly.**
Torres/Ribeiro/Duarte/Cabri 2010 (*Phys Ther Sport*, general
lower-limb eccentric protocol) reported that position-sense
impairment was present only immediately post-exercise (attributed to
fatigue), while impairment of the reaction angle to release persisted
up to **3 days** post-exercise (attributed to muscle damage). This
matches the DOMS 24–72 h peak window and is one of the few pieces of
evidence that the proprioceptive component of DOMS is *not* just
same-session fatigue but extends into the recovery days.

**Bottom line for F1 on this axis.** The claim "acute peroneal DOMS
reduces proprioceptive threshold at the ankle" is **mechanistically
plausible with mixed evidence** — isolated peroneal fatigue is often
null on JPS, whole-limb fatigue shows SDM ≈ 0.5 acutely, and
eccentric-with-DOMS shows proprioceptive impairment that persists
into the 3-day window. The magnitude is not large in absolute terms
and no study has done the exact peroneal-DOMS × JPS-time-course
experiment.

### 4. CAI-specific amplification under fatigue-analogue stress

**CAI subjects lose their protective compensation under fatigue.**
Jeon et al. 2025 (*J Sports Sci Med*,
[PMC11877301](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11877301/),
N = 20 CAI + 20 lateral-ankle-sprain copers + 20 controls) fatigued
subjects via a cycle of directional running, side hopping, cone jumps
and tuck jumps to RPE ≥ 17 and HR ≥ 85 % maximal (achieved 96.1 % HRmax
mean). Non-fatigued CAI subjects up-regulated peroneal activation to
protect the ankle; **under fatigue the protective compensation
disappeared**: CAI subjects showed decreased peroneal neuromuscular
control pre- and post-landing, decreased ankle eversion moment,
reduced ankle abduction angle, stiffer landing at the proximal joint,
and "biomechanics associated with ankle injuries". Copers showed the
opposite: an *increased* eversion moment early in landing.
Authors' verbatim: *"Under fatigue conditions, patients with CAI
exhibited biomechanical changes in the proximal joint, a stiffer
landing position, and biomechanics associated with ankle injuries."*

**Fatigue as a real intrinsic risk modifier.** Willems 2005b (*Am J
Sports Med*, N = 241 male PE students followed 1–3 y) prospectively
identified **decreased dorsiflexor strength, decreased dorsiflexion
range of motion, less balance, poor coordination** as intrinsic
risk factors for inversion sprain. de Noronha 2006 (*BJSM*, systematic
review of longitudinal studies) identified reduced dorsiflexion
range, postural sway, and (possibly) proprioception as predictors;
voluntary strength did **not** predict occurrence on its own. Taken
together, the intrinsic-risk literature confirms that the *state* of
the neuromuscular system at the moment of exposure matters — and
fatigue moves subjects toward the risk profile.

**Weighting for the coach decision.** Jeon 2025 is the closest
piece of direct evidence for the CAI-amplification claim: a
running-based fatigue protocol (not DOMS specifically) produced a
divergent landing mechanic between copers (protective) and CAI
(un-protective). Extrapolating from "fatigue during exercise" to
"DOMS the next day" is not identical, but it moves the confidence
above pure mechanism. For a **CAI-history athlete** with acute
peroneal DOMS, the protective-compensation-loss finding is the
strongest single anchor for the terrain restriction; for a
**non-CAI athlete**, the direct evidence is thinner.

### 5. From mechanism to sprain risk — what the caution buys, what it does not

The chain "peroneal DOMS → longer reflex latency + weaker eversion →
higher inversion-sprain risk" is the intuitive causal story, but the
evidence in sections 1–3 shows two important qualifications:

1. **The reflex is too slow at baseline; feed-forward is the actual
   protection axis.** DOMS-induced changes to reflex latency do not
   convert a protected event into an unprotected one. What DOMS
   plausibly degrades is **motor-strategy quality** (pre-activation,
   step selection, stiffness pre-setting) — a component that the
   framework's balance research already identifies as the wirksame
   Achse
   ([balance-static-hold-vs-perturbation-ankle-sprain-prevention.md](balance-static-hold-vs-perturbation-ankle-sprain-prevention.md)
   § 2e).
2. **The direct empirical claim "acute peroneal DOMS increases sprain
   incidence" has no prospective RCT.** The chain is defensible as
   mechanism-plus-analogue (fatigue → EMD +10–15 ms; CAI under
   fatigue → protective-compensation loss), but the size of the
   sprain-incidence effect is unmeasured.

**What the caution actually buys.** The dominant risk-modifier under
the coach's control during acute DOMS is **exposure reduction** — the
*number of inversion trigger events per training minute*. On flat
ground the trigger rate is close to zero; on unstable / uneven
terrain it scales with terrain roughness and pace. This is *exposure
epidemiology*, not muscle physiology, and it is where the flat-ground
restriction earns its keep. The mechanism-side gain (a partially
weakened defense at each trigger event) is a real but smaller add-on.

**Walk-out in the fatigue phase.** Two overlapping supports:
(a) intra-session fatigue independently lengthens EMD (Flevas 2022);
(b) CAI subjects lose the protective landing compensation under a
run-based fatigue protocol (Jeon 2025). Walking eliminates the
impact-loading phase where the sprain events cluster; observational
reporting on marathon events points to mile-22-type "misstep"
patterns as the modal timing. The evidence class is one step below
RCT (mechanism + observational + one CAI × fatigue interaction
study), but it aligns.

**Race pace vs. easy pace.** Higher ground-reaction forces and shorter
ground-contact times leave less time for corrective adjustment — but
this is the *same argument* that concludes the reflex is too slow to
save the sprain in the first place. What race pace changes is (a) the
feed-forward window becomes more automatic and less consciously
correctable, (b) exposure minutes at high-consequence combinations
(unstable terrain × high force × cognitive load) accumulate faster.
There is **no direct RCT** comparing sprain incidence at race pace
vs. easy pace on the same terrain. The mechanism-based caution
against race-pace blocks on trail while acute peroneal DOMS is
present is defensible but under-quantified.

**Relative efficacy of hebel — how this doc updates the balance-training
picture.** The existing framework research
([balance-static-hold-vs-perturbation-ankle-sprain-prevention.md](balance-static-hold-vs-perturbation-ankle-sprain-prevention.md))
establishes that perturbation-based training is the wirksame Mechanismus
for building the ankle's inversion defense over weeks. **This document
does not shift that picture.** What it clarifies is the timescale
separation: perturbation training builds *capacity* over 6–12 weeks;
in the acute DOMS window (24–72 h) the same capacity is
transiently reduced and cannot be re-built by a same-day session.
The hebel with the largest acute effect during that window is
**terrain choice + external stützung** (tape / brace / stable shoe),
not any short-term training intervention. This matches the pattern
found in
[cai-perturbation-training-programming.md](cai-perturbation-training-programming.md)
(§ Brace + neuromuskuläres Training, TL;DR): acute protection = brace
+ terrain; capacity = perturbation training over weeks.

### 6. Operative synthesis for F1 — terrain, walk-out, cadence, race pace

The evidence supports the coach's conservative default with the
qualifications spelled out in section 5. Concretely:

| Athlete profile | Terrain during acute evertor DOMS (24–72 h) | Fatigue-phase auslauf | Race-pace block within 72 h |
|---|---|---|---|
| **CAI-history (recurrent lateral sprain)** | Flat ground default (strongest single anchor: Jeon 2025 loss of protective landing compensation under fatigue). Uneven / technical terrain only with external stützung (tape/brace) and shortened session. | Walk-out from the moment moderate lower-leg fatigue is felt — the strategy-loss finding is CAI-specific and stacks with intra-session peroneal EMD lengthening. | Skip during the peak DOMS window (24–72 h). Release only when normal walking is pain-free and the shin-bone palpation test (§ 7) is negative. |
| **No CAI history, first-time exposure** | Flat ground *preferred* but not mechanistically mandatory. The strongest justification collapses to exposure reduction, not per-event defense; a technical run is not contraindicated but a **shortened first exposure** in that window is safer than the usual session length. | Walk-out advised in the last 10–15 % of the session if any lingering symptom. Otherwise not mandatory. | Skip within 24 h of onset (strength deficit + running-economy inflation both peak here). 48–72 h out: release conditional on the same pain-free-walk + shin-bone-palpation test, plus explicit first-block RPE gate (abort if the target pace requires > 1 RPE point above the type-history norm). |

**Time-window ceiling.** The 48–72 h "no uneven terrain" default
aligns with the DOMS peak window (24–72 h) plus a safety margin into
the day-3 repair-onset boundary. For simple flip-flop-style DOMS the
48 h ceiling is usually adequate; the 72 h ceiling is the more
conservative CAI variant. Beyond 72 h with symptoms still present,
the working assumption shifts from "DOMS" to "something else" and the
F2 differential (section 7) becomes primary.

**Cadence / step-length correction is not a same-day lever.** The
running-cadence literature (framework:
[running-cadence-myths.md](running-cadence-myths.md)) shows +5 %
cadence as a modest injury-modifier over training weeks, not an
acute-DOMS intervention. Do not stack a cadence prescription onto a
DOMS-day workout.

### 7. F2 — differentiating exertional lower-leg DOMS from beginning MTSS / CECS

**Anatomical grounding.** Flip-flops / thong sandals require elevated
tibialis anterior activation in terminal swing (to lift the sandal
against gravity so the toe of the sandal does not scuff the ground)
plus toe-flexor / peroneus-tertius involvement to retain the sandal
on the foot. Documented biomechanical changes: increased maximum
ankle dorsiflexion in swing, increased tibialis anterior EMG in
terminal swing, no consistent increase in co-contraction indices
(Chen 2018 *PLoS ONE* 13(3):e0193653; Zhang 2013 *Gait Posture* on
flip-flop dorsiflexion). The mechanism produces soreness in the
**anterior-lateral** muscle chain: tibialis anterior, extensor
digitorum longus, peroneus tertius, and peroneus longus in its
sagittal-plane role (dorsiflexion assist) plus its frontal-plane role
(eversion).

**MTSS is a different tissue and a different location.** MTSS involves
**bone / periosteum stress** at the posteromedial tibial border,
driven by repetitive tension from the tibialis posterior, soleus and
FDL — a **posteromedial** muscle group (StatPearls MTSS chapter;
Winkelmann 2016 "MTSS: Muscles Located at the Site of Pain"
[PMC4811262](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4811262/)).
Anatomically the DOMS distribution and the MTSS distribution do not
overlap. The three criteria that separate them in the field:

| Feature | Simple lower-leg DOMS | MTSS |
|---|---|---|
| **Site of pain reproduction** | Muscle belly — pressing the tibialis anterior belly or the peroneal group reproduces the pain; pressing the medial edge of the tibia bone does **not** | **Bone edge** — pressing along the posteromedial tibial border reproduces the pain over a diffuse ≥ 5 cm segment; the muscle belly may or may not be tender |
| **Warm-up pattern** | Little change; may improve slightly with movement in a generic way | Improves during warm-up, often disappears mid-run, returns / worsens after |
| **Day-to-day trend** | Improving day-over-day from the peak; gone by day 5–7 | Worsening or stalled day-over-day; morning discomfort persists / grows |
| **Time course from provocation** | Onset 12–24 h after the specific bout | Emerges after a rapid increase in training frequency / duration / intensity; gradual over sessions |

**CECS is the third possibility to rule out.** StatPearls CECS
(*NBK544284*): pain begins predictably 15–20 min into exertional
activity; described as *"squeezing, cramping, aching, or burning"*;
resolves quickly after activity stops; bilateral in 70–95 % of cases;
may include paresthesias / weakness. The single most useful clinical
marker is the **onset predictability with exertion time and the fast
post-exertion resolution** — DOMS and MTSS both fail to match that
pattern. CECS is the most common initial misdiagnosis for MTSS, so
"cramping / tight / burning that arrives at a predictable minute of a
run and goes away within minutes of stopping" flips the differential
away from both DOMS and MTSS toward CECS and needs
sports-med-ortho referral rather than a training decision.

**Athlete-answerable differential (one to two sentences in chat):**

1. **Point-tenderness question.** "Press along the inner edge of the
   shin bone from mid-shin down to just above the ankle — is that
   painful over a stretch of about 5 cm? Or only the muscle in front
   of / behind the bone is sore?"
   - Bone-edge tenderness ≥ 5 cm → treat as MTSS trajectory
     (deferred tempo, StatPearls return-to-run cascade).
   - Muscle-belly only → simple DOMS (return-to-tempo cascade below).
2. **Warm-up pattern question.** "When you start moving in the
   morning, does the pain fade after 5–10 minutes of warm-up and then
   come back worse afterwards, or does it stay roughly the same?"
   - Fade-and-return-worse → MTSS trajectory.
   - Stays the same / gradually eases → DOMS.
3. **Neurologic / cramping question (only if the first two are
   ambiguous).** "During a run, do you get a squeezing / burning /
   cramping feeling that starts at a predictable time and goes away
   within a few minutes of stopping? Any tingling or numbness on the
   top of the foot?"
   - Yes → CECS differential, refer, do not run tempo.
   - No → stay with DOMS / MTSS branches.

### 8. Return-to-tempo criteria after acute lower-leg DOMS (F2 continued)

**For simple DOMS.** No evidence supports deferring tempo beyond the
biomechanically compromised window. The literature carries three
constraints that shape the release:

1. **Running economy is impaired at ≥ 85 % VO₂max under recent EIMD.**
   Chen 2007 (*J Strength Cond Res*) and derived reviews show that
   easy-aerobic economy is largely unaffected by mild DOMS, but tempo
   / race-pace effort inflates VO₂ cost — the finding replicates
   across downhill-run and unaccustomed-eccentric protocols. A tempo
   session in the 24 h post-onset window therefore reads elevated
   RPE at target pace and is not a clean progression datapoint.
2. **Stride mechanics change under DOMS.** Reviewer summaries note
   "muscle damage led to changes in stride mechanics and a greater
   reliance on anaerobic methods of energy production, contributing
   to the change in running economy during DOMS", with the change in
   oxygen consumption inversely correlated with stride-length change.
   A tempo block on altered mechanics is a substrate for compensation
   injury.
3. **Strength deficit lags soreness recovery.** MVIC recovers faster
   than soreness in low-deficit cases (< 20 % → ~2 d) and slower in
   high-deficit cases (≈ 50 % → up to 7 d). For flip-flop-style DOMS
   the deficit is typically in the moderate band; a 48–72 h defer of
   tempo covers the peak-loss window.

**Practical release heuristic for simple DOMS:**
- ≤ 24 h post-onset: no tempo / race-pace stimulus. Easy movement
  and short easy running are safe and can *help* DOMS via blood
  flow (Cheung 2003).
- 24–72 h (peak window): tempo held. Easy Z2 permitted at reduced
  volume if walking is pain-free.
- **Release criteria at 48–72 h**: (a) normal walking pain-free;
  (b) shin-bone-palpation test negative over the full 5 cm
  posteromedial tibial border; (c) no CECS-pattern cramping /
  burning at exertion onset in a preceding easy run; (d) in-session
  first-block gate — if target pace requires > 1 RPE point above
  the type-history norm in the first block, abort and re-attempt
  ≥ 48 h later.

**For MTSS trajectory** (bone-edge tenderness present): defer tempo
until the StatPearls return-to-run cascade completes — pain-free
daily activities for 2 consecutive days → pain-free 30 min walk →
bone tenderness resolved ≥ 1 week → **week-1** 30-second run blocks
at 30–50 % usual pace alternating with walking → weeks 2–5 gradual
increase with rest days → after 5 weeks 10 %/week distance build →
speed only after full easy-volume return. If pain re-appears at rest,
drop back one level. This is a **weeks-scale** deferral, not a
same-week release, and it belongs in the coach's decision path only
after the physio-/ortho-consultant has confirmed the diagnosis.

**No trajectory marker beats symptom monitoring.** The literature has
no validated feld-marker (running dynamics, HR at fixed pace,
subjective RPE) that predicts MTSS conversion from initial soreness.
Imaging (MRI) can differentiate DOMS from MTSS periosteal edema and
MTSS from stress fracture, but it is not part of the coach's
day-to-day toolkit. **Daily symptom monitoring + the three
differential questions in section 7 are the state of the art.**

## Open questions / Caveats

1. **No prospective RCT of "acute peroneal DOMS → sprain incidence".**
   The causal chain rests on mechanism (Konradsen 1997), fatigue
   analogue (Flevas 2022, Han 2024), and CAI-fatigue interaction
   (Jeon 2025). A trial that assigns CAI subjects to a
   flip-flop-style DOMS induction vs. control and measures sprain
   incidence in the next 72 h on standardized uneven terrain would
   settle the question. It does not exist.
2. **Feed-forward pre-activation under DOMS is unmeasured.** The
   feed-forward pathway is where the ankle's inversion protection
   actually lives (Konradsen 1997, Hayek 2023 in
   [balance-static-hold-vs-perturbation-ankle-sprain-prevention.md](balance-static-hold-vs-perturbation-ankle-sprain-prevention.md)
   § 2e). Whether acute peroneal DOMS reduces the amplitude of
   pre-landing peroneal activation on step-down / mis-step onto an
   uneven surface has not been directly tested — Hayek 2023 measured
   an *age* effect on pre-activation, not a DOMS effect. This is the
   most decision-relevant single missing experiment.
3. **Peroneal-specific eversion-torque deficit after DOMS is not
   quantified.** All eversion-torque loss numbers used in the
   operative section are generic-EIMD extrapolations; no study has
   done selective peroneal DOMS induction + MVIC time course.
4. **"Fatigue" and "DOMS" are not interchangeable.** Flevas 2022
   (concentric fatigue) and Jeon 2025 (session-end fatigue) are the
   two direct pieces of evidence and both measure fatigue during /
   immediately after exercise, not DOMS 24–72 h later. Extrapolation
   is defensible on shared mechanism (strength deficit, altered
   neural drive) but the DOMS-day peroneal-EMD change and
   DOMS-day CAI landing-mechanic change have not been directly
   measured.
5. **CAI heterogeneity.** "CAI" covers mechanical, functional, and
   perceived-instability subtypes. Jeon 2025 categorized by IdFAI
   criteria; whether the protective-compensation loss under fatigue
   is uniform across subtypes is unresolved.
6. **The Vaes 2002 null finding on baseline peroneal latency in CAI
   is inconsistent with several other reports.** The literature on
   whether CAI carries a longer resting reflex latency is split; the
   safer reading is that CAI shows altered activation timing in
   functional tasks (landing, transitions, single-limb stance)
   rather than a longer laboratory reflex latency.
7. **The 5 cm bedside palpation criterion for MTSS is a case-series
   convention, not a validated diagnostic test.** StatPearls treats
   it as a working clinical criterion; MRI remains the reference
   standard for MTSS confirmation and for separating MTSS from a
   beginning stress fracture. The bedside test is a *routing* test
   for the coach, not a *diagnosis*.
8. **CECS bedside differentiation without compartment-pressure
   measurement is unreliable.** The onset-timing + fast-resolution
   + paresthesia pattern is highly suggestive but does not replace a
   compartment-pressure test. Suspected CECS is a sports-ortho
   referral, not a coach decision.
9. **No trajectory marker for MTSS conversion from initial soreness
   has been validated for the field.** The coach's tool is daily
   symptom monitoring + the § 7 questions. Any claim that a running
   dynamics metric (GCT, VO, vertical stiffness) predicts MTSS
   conversion is speculative and not evidence-based at the current
   state of the literature.
